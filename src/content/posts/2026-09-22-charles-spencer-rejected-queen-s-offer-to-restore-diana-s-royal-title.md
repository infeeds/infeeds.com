Charles Spencer has opened up about a pivotal moment following Princess Diana's death, revealing he made the difficult choice to reject Queen Elizabeth's offer to restore his sister's "Her Royal Highness" title. The disclosure comes from Spencer's upcoming memoir "Swan Song: Diana, My Sister," which provides an intimate look at the royal family's handling of Diana's legacy.

## A Gesture Made Too Late

According to excerpts published by the Daily Mail, Spencer was traveling on a train carrying Diana's coffin to Althorp, the Spencer family estate where Diana is buried. During this solemn journey, Robert Fellowes, the Queen's secretary and Spencer's brother-in-law, approached him with an extraordinary message.

"I have just received a call from Her Majesty," Fellowes reportedly said. "She would like to offer Diana her HRH title back."

The "Her Royal Highness" designation had been stripped from Diana following her 1996 divorce from Prince Charles, a decision that reportedly wounded the princess deeply. At the time, she became known simply as "Diana, Princess of Wales" rather than maintaining her royal styling.

## The Weight of the Decision

Spencer described his immediate internal struggle upon hearing the Queen's offer. He found himself asking what Diana herself would have wanted in that moment. The historian reflected on how the original removal of the title had felt "petty and vindictive," yet he couldn't shake the feeling that offering it posthumously missed the point entirely.

"That's very kind of the Queen, of course. But what's the point, Robert? Now? I mean, Diana's dead," Spencer recalled saying, according to his memoir. The response captures both the absurdity and the heartbreak of the moment.

Yet Spencer has since questioned whether he made the right call. In his book, he acknowledges that Diana might have appreciated the symbolic gesture, or that her sons, Prince William and Prince Harry, might have found comfort in their father's mother honoring their mother this way. However, in that moment, Spencer felt primarily protective of his late sister and unable to see the offer as anything but a consolation prize.

## A Missed Opportunity?

The most biting critique comes in Spencer's reflection that the title should never have been removed during Diana's lifetime in the first place. By that logic, there would have been no need to offer it posthumously. The removal itself, not its restoration, remains the real point of contention.

Buckingham Palace's response to Spencer's revelations is notably pointed, with a spokesperson suggesting that "the pain of fraternal grief can cloud reason, affect judgment and color memory." This pushback underscores how contested Diana's legacy remains within [royal circles](https://infeeds.com/tags/entertainment), even decades after her tragic death.

Prince Harry, Diana's younger son, has remained notably silent on his uncle's book. When approached by a reporter about Spencer's memoir on Thursday, Harry declined to engage with questions about the subject, redirecting his attention to those around him.

## The Ongoing Debate

Spencer's account raises uncomfortable questions about royal protocol, family loyalty, and the ways institutions handle [loss and remembrance](https://infeeds.com/tags/royalty). Was restoring Diana's title an olive branch or a publicity move? Could accepting it have helped heal family wounds or would it have felt like accepting an inadequate apology for years of perceived slights?

These questions may never be fully answered, but Spencer's willingness to revisit his decision in print suggests they continue to weigh on him. The memoir offers a rare window into the private conversations and ethical dilemmas that occur behind palace doors, reminding us that even in moments of profound grief, there are complicated choices that can never be unmade.
