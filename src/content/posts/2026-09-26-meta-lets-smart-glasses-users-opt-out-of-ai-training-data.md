Meta is finally giving smart glasses users some control over their data. The company has rolled out a new setting that lets people opt out of allowing Meta to use visual data collected by their glasses for AI training purposes. If you own a pair of Meta smart glasses, you've probably already seen the notification pop up in your Meta AI app explaining this change.

## Taking Control of Your Visual Data

The opt-out process is straightforward, though it requires a bit of navigation. You'll need to head into your glasses settings, select "Meta AI," and scroll down to "store visual data from AI experiences." One catch: your glasses need to be out of their case and connected to your phone to make the change. If you own multiple pairs, you'll need to repeat this for each one.

Meta's help center article provides more transparency about how the company actually uses this visual data. The company collects it when you use AI features like asking about something you're looking at or using live AI. Here's where it gets interesting: Meta admits that this data may be reviewed by humans, not just processed automatically. "This includes the process of training and improving AI models, which may be automated or manual (human)," the company states.

For many users, this admission will raise eyebrows. The possibility of human reviewers examining your visual data, even if anonymized, feels invasive to some. Yet Meta is being relatively honest about it, which is more than we've seen from some tech companies in the past.

## Understanding the Tradeoffs

Here's the caveat that might make you think twice before opting out: certain features won't work if you disable visual data storage. Most notably, "visual reminders" disappear. That means you won't be able to look at something and ask Meta AI to remind you to buy it later. Meta hasn't been entirely clear about whether other features are affected, which is frustrating given the importance of informed consent.

It's worth noting that Meta distinguishes between "visual data" and actual photos or videos. The visual data setting doesn't apply to photos and videos you capture when the capture LED is on. That's a meaningful distinction, though it also highlights how much data collection is happening silently in the background when you're not actively recording.

## The Bigger Picture

This move from Meta arrives amid growing scrutiny of how tech companies handle personal data for [AI training](https://infeeds.com/tags/ai). Privacy advocates have increasingly pushed back against using people's photos and videos to train models without explicit consent. Meta's new setting is a response to that pressure, though whether it goes far enough remains debatable.

The fact that Meta is even offering this choice is notable in [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) policy terms. Many companies still collect and use data for AI training with minimal transparency. But the feature limitations attached to opting out create a troubling dynamic: you can have privacy, but it'll cost you functionality.

Meta's approach here walks a fine line. The company is being more transparent than before and giving users agency, but it's also creating incentives to keep data sharing enabled. For privacy-conscious users, the tradeoff might be worth it. For others, the convenience of visual reminders will likely win out.

The real question is whether this represents genuine progress or just another layer of privacy theater designed to satisfy regulators while maintaining Meta's data advantage. Either way, users now have a choice, which is more than they had before.
