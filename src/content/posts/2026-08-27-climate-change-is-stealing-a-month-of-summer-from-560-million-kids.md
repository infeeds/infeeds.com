It's 8:52 AM and already 86 degrees Fahrenheit in Southern California. My four-month-old is inside, away from the heat. Outside, an extreme heat warning glares from my phone: temperatures climbing to 110 degrees, stay indoors, stay out of the sun. This is becoming normal for millions of families.

According to new research published in Science Advances, around 560 million children under age 10 are now experiencing at least one extra month of heat stress each year because of climate change. That's not hyperbole. That's not some distant future scenario. That's happening right now.

## The Data Behind the Heat

Rosa Pietroiusti, lead author of the study and a PhD researcher at Vrije Universiteit Brussel, explains that her team used an international standard for moderate heat stress. They looked at days when protective measures like rest breaks become necessary to prevent heat-related illness. The threshold is set at a WetBulb Globe Temperature of 28 degrees Celsius, a measure accounting for heat, humidity, wind speed, and cloud cover.

The findings are stark: 43 percent of all kids in the 0-9 age group are experiencing climate-induced heat stress. That's nearly triple the number of people aged 60-69 exposed to the same amount of additional heat. Children are disproportionately vulnerable, yet they're also the least responsible for the [climate crisis](https://infeeds.com/tags/climate) we've created.

The cruelest part? This burden falls heaviest on countries that contributed least to fossil fuel pollution. Nations in the Global South, which have younger populations and less access to air conditioning or heat-resistant infrastructure, face the highest increases in heat stress. If we overshoot the Paris climate accord targets by just 2 degrees Celsius, that number jumps to 650 million children.

## Why Kids Are Uniquely Vulnerable

Babies and young children aren't just smaller versions of adults. Their bodies work differently in dangerous ways when temperatures spike. They have a higher surface area-to-body mass ratio, which means they overheat faster, like a small ice cube melting in the sun compared to a big block. Their sweat glands are still developing, making temperature regulation nearly impossible without adult intervention.

Heat doesn't arrive with the dramatic warnings of a hurricane or tornado. It sneaks up quietly. Yet according to the World Health Organization, it's a leading cause of weather-related deaths globally. Heat stress causes dehydration, heat stroke, and eventually organ failure. It's preventable if kids can find somewhere cool, but what happens when entire regions lack reliable air conditioning or power?

My own experience this summer shows how [technology](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) hasn't solved this problem. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends keeping babies indoors when the 'feels like' temperature exceeds 90 degrees Fahrenheit. Some days, that meant my family was stuck inside from 11 AM until past sunset. Outdoor time, crucial for child development and parental mental health, became dangerous.

## When Infrastructure Fails

We lost power one hot July afternoon. One afternoon. I had to pack up and leave because the air conditioning went out and I was terrified about the breastmilk stored in my freezer. That represented 20 hours of my time pumping, much of it during sleepless nights. We were lucky to have somewhere to go. Others don't have that option.

Power outages are growing longer across the US as extreme weather intensifies. Heatwaves trigger blackouts because everyone blasts AC simultaneously, or because utility companies preemptively shut off lines to prevent wildfires. Families without backup plans, without resources, without air conditioning at all, face genuine danger.

I had daydreamed about lazy summer days at the beach with my newborn. We made it to the ocean for a few minutes. My baby wore a little blue bucket hat because his skin is too sensitive for sunscreen until 6 months old. When I tried to show him the water, the bright sun hit his eyes and he scowled. "This is too much?" I asked. He fussed. "This is too much."

For 560 million children, every summer now feels like too much.
