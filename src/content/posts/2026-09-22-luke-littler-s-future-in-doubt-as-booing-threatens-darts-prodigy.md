Luke Littler has achieved more by 19 than most athletes accomplish in a lifetime. World champion, multi-millionaire, TV personality, and the undisputed number one player in professional darts. Yet despite all this success, the Warrington native is seriously contemplating leaving the sport altogether.

The trigger? Relentless booing and whistles from sections of the crowd at tournaments across Europe and the UK.

## When Fans Turn Against Success

This past weekend at the World Series of Darts in Amsterdam, Littler was once again subjected to a hostile reception as he attempted to become the first player ever to win all 10 major televised tournaments in a calendar year. The atmosphere was so charged that a referee actually stopped play during his match against Nathan Aspinall to warn the crowd about their behaviour.

Littler has since withdrawn from upcoming events, stating bluntly: "Just no point in me travelling, wasting my time while I'm getting booed." He's even suggested he won't return to tournaments in the Netherlands if the treatment continues.

This isn't an isolated incident. The teenager has faced similar treatment at venues across the UK, and Samuel Gill, editor of Dartnews.com, is deeply concerned about the long-term consequences. "There's a real fear that within a few years he'll just turn around and walk away from the sport," Gill told BBC Sport.

## A Pattern That Started With Van Gerwen

Surprisingly, this dynamic isn't unique to Littler. Michael van Gerwen, the Dutch legend who dominated darts for years, experienced identical treatment when he was winning everything in sight. Fans wanted someone different to emerge, someone to break his stranglehold on the sport.

"Everyone wanted to boo him," Gill explained. "It's something different for the sport every single week." The irony is striking: Littler's extraordinary dominance, the very thing that should make him a [sports](https://infeeds.com/tags/sports) icon, is precisely what's driving crowds against him.

## What Darts Stands to Lose

Here's where it gets serious. Littler isn't just another talented player. He's transforming darts. Tickets to his events sell out instantly. Applications to darts academies are at all-time highs. He's become back-page news, transcending the sport and bringing it to mainstream audiences like never before.

If he walks away, the damage could be catastrophic. The sport has built enormous momentum around his presence, and losing that could set [sports](https://infeeds.com/tags/sports) back years. "He's the one who is bringing this sport to a completely different level," Gill said. "If Littler was to walk away, the sport would be the real loser."

## The Character Question

Karl Holden, secretary of St Helen's Darts Academy where Littler trained from age nine, offers a different perspective. "They don't like him because he's that good. And he's got a bit of swagger to him," Holden said. "Luke's got a bit of character to him. You need that, otherwise darts would be totally boring."

There's something refreshing about Littler's confidence. In a sport that often rewards showmanship and personality, he delivers exactly that. Yet it seems some fans resent his success more than they appreciate his entertainment value.

## The Money Complicates Everything

The financial reality makes this crisis even more acute. Littler is already wealthy enough to retire comfortably. He doesn't need darts anymore. The sport needs him. Gill estimates that if the booing continues, Littler could walk away within four to five years, potentially at just 24 or 25 years old.

"He might just decide 'I just want a quiet life now and just live off his money'," Gill suggested.

What's truly remarkable is that we're even having this conversation about a teenager who turned professional just two years ago and has already won almost every major trophy. But that's the reality when someone is so talented, so dominant, and so polarizing.

The challenge for Littler is navigating a paradox: maintaining his victorious streak while keeping fans engaged, even though some of them are explicitly paying to watch him lose.
