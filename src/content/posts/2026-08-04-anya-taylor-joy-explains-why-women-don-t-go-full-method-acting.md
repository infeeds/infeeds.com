Anya Taylor-Joy has delivered powerhouse performances across "The Witch," "The Queen's Gambit," and "Furiosa," but she's never fully committed to Method acting and doesn't plan to start. In a recent viral interview with Complex News, the acclaimed actress offered a refreshingly candid take on why women in [entertainment](https://infeeds.com/tags/entertainment) largely avoid the extreme performance technique that's become synonymous with male actors.

"If you'll notice, women don't method act because we have things to take care of, so we can't completely lose our minds," Taylor-Joy said. She's not wrong. The gap between how Method acting is discussed and practiced across gender lines is striking, and Taylor-Joy's observation cuts right to the heart of it.

## The Controlled Psychosis of Acting

Taylor-Joy describes acting itself as "a controlled psychosis in a way." She explains that the craft involves pretending to be someone else, inhabiting their thoughts for 16 hours a day, moving like them, living in their clothes and spaces. It's like having an uninvited roommate lodging in your mind temporarily. Most actors understand this psychological space naturally.

But there's a crucial difference between method acting and what Taylor-Joy does. She has safeguards. Her inner circle knows when the character work gets too intense. They'll call her by the character's name and tell her they need to "check in again." She's built a system of accountability.

"I can't imagine keeping it up all the time because you're going to work with hundreds of people and it is your responsibility to be good to them and to help them do their best job," she explained. This responsibility, this baseline expectation of emotional labor and professionalism, is precisely what separates her approach from full-blown Method acting.

## A Gender Dynamics Issue

Kristen Stewart echoed these sentiments just months earlier when speaking with The New York Times. She framed acting itself as "quite embarrassing and un-masculine" by nature, suggesting that Method acting has become a way for male performers to armor themselves against the vulnerability that performance requires. "Performance is inherently vulnerable and therefore quite embarrassing and unmasculine," Stewart said. "There's no bravado in suggesting that you're a mouthpiece for someone else's ideas. It's inherently submissive."

Stewart posed a pointed question: "Have you ever heard of a female actor that was method?" The answer is essentially no. There's a reason for that, and it goes beyond mere coincidence or preference. Women rarely have the social permission to completely check out emotionally and psychologically on set. The expectation of emotional availability, warmth, and collaboration remains constant.

## The Other Side of the Debate

Meanwhile, Daniel Day-Lewis spent much of his 2025 press tour defending Method acting itself. The legendary actor bristled at recent criticism of the technique, calling much of the commentary uninformed. "All the recent commentary in the last few years about Method acting is invariably from people who have little or no understanding of what it actually involves," he said at the London Film Festival.

Day-Lewis argues that [Method acting](https://infeeds.com/tags/acting) is simply a tool for "freeing yourself so you present your colleagues with a living, breathing human being they can interact with." He's frustrated by the sensationalism surrounding the technique, tired of hearing it described as "lunacy." To him, it's no more unusual than staying focused on a character between takes rather than cracking jokes or pulling pranks.

"It's very simple," he stressed to Big Issue. "So it pisses me off this whole 'Oh, he went full Method' thing."

Yet the irony remains: when a man does this, it's called dedication. When a woman suggests she needs similar psychological space, she's questioned about her professionalism and her obligations to others. Taylor-Joy's honest assessment cuts through the pretense. She's not saying Method acting doesn't work. She's saying women don't have the luxury of attempting it without facing social and professional consequences that men simply don't encounter.
