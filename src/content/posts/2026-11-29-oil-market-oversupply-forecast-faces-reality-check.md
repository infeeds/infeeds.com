## A Sharp Reversal in Oil Markets

Crude oil has experienced a dramatic November selloff, with prices tumbling roughly 20% from their October peaks. Brent futures dropped from $118 a barrel to $95.44, while West Texas Intermediate fell from $104 to $83.08. The catalyst appears straightforward: investors believe Middle East exports are recovering enough to tip the market into surplus for the first time since the Iran conflict began.

Retail gasoline prices have benefited from this decline, now trading well below $4 per gallon, with diesel easing below $6. On the surface, this looks like relief for consumers and a normalization after months of geopolitical uncertainty.

But experts are divided on whether this optimism is warranted. The International Energy Agency has forecasted an oil surplus beginning in the fourth quarter, while Goldman Sachs predicts a 2 million barrel-per-day surplus by 2027. Commodity tracking firm Kpler sees even larger oversupply at 3 million bpd next year.

## The Cautionary Tale

Yet seasoned energy analysts are pumping the brakes. Giovanni Staunovo at UBS warns that the market has become "too comfortable with the supply outlook" and that the rebound in Gulf exports shouldn't be mistaken for true normalization. David Goldwyn, the Obama administration's former special envoy for international energy, was blunt about surplus predictions: "If pigs had wings, they'd fly."

The problem isn't what's happening now. It's what could happen next. Middle East crude exports remain about 24% below pre-war levels at 12.8 million barrels per day, compared to 17 million before the conflict. Fuel shipments are down nearly 50%. These figures suggest the region is still far from normal.

Attacks on commercial shipping near the Strait of Hormuz continue at a near-daily pace, with 20 incidents reported just this month. While October saw 35 attacks, the decline doesn't necessarily signal improving security so much as a temporary lull.

## Political Uncertainty Looms

There's also the Trump variable. The president recently told Fox News he might launch renewed strikes on Iran after the new year. Such escalation could send crude prices well above $100 again, according to Bob McNally, president of Rapidan Energy. McNally expects oil to trade in a wide $70-$80 range during calm periods, but spike dramatically during conflict intensification.

Iran would likely retaliate against U.S. military action by targeting ports, refineries, or processing facilities. A single strike on the UAE's Fujairah export terminal or Saudi infrastructure at Ras Tanura or Yanbu could flip the surplus scenario into deficit territory overnight. And this would occur when global [energy](https://infeeds.com/tags/energy) inventories are already at historic lows.

There's limited room to maneuver. The U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve could potentially release another 100 million barrels, and Europe has refined product reserves available. But neither the U.S. nor European governments will be eager to tap these reserves again simply for price relief, particularly if the Middle East situation becomes chronic.

## A Fragile Recovery

One bright spot comes from China, which is increasing crude imports as Middle East tensions persist. Beijing's decision to cut imports dramatically earlier this year helped prevent prices from spiking even higher. This renewed demand, combined with seasonal factors and refinery maintenance, is currently supporting the bearish oil view.

Price forecasts for 2027 vary wildly. JPMorgan sees Brent trading in the high $70s during the first half of next year, dipping into the $60s later. Goldman Sachs is more bullish, forecasting $88 Brent and $78 WTI. The massive spread reflects genuine uncertainty about whether geopolitical risk will dominate or [business](https://infeeds.com/tags/business) fundamentals will hold.

The November selloff has been dramatic, but it's built on an assumption that may not survive the first real security incident in the Persian Gulf. The oil market's comfort level with surplus scenarios might prove to be just another casualty of geopolitical volatility.

When history judges this energy market cycle, it may well ask which forecasters understood that peaceful markets are exceptions in the Middle East, not the rule.
