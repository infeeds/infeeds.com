The Oak Ridge Boys left an indelible mark on American music, bridging gospel traditions with country and pop sensibilities across five decades. When Richard Sterban passed away in November at 83, the world lost one of the most distinctive bass voices in vocal group history. But his legacy, along with bandmates Duane Allen, William Lee Golden, and Joe Bonsall, lives on through a catalog that defined what it meant to be a crossover act in the modern era.

The group's journey began in Knoxville, Tennessee during the 1940s, when they emerged as the Oak Ridge Quintet singing gospel material. That sacred foundation never left them, even as they pivoted to country and pop throughout the 1970s and 1980s. Their casual-sharp aesthetic and musically pliant approach made these transitions feel natural rather than forced. They appeared on collaborations with superstars like Paul Simon, bringing their ethereal harmonies to "Slip Slidin' Away" with a grace that few contemporary acts could match.

## Crossover Smashes That Defined an Era

"Elvira" remains their defining moment. Released in 1981, the song's jovial gait, horn section, and unforgettable hook delivered by Sterban's well-deep bass became inescapable. "Giddy up, um-poppa-um-poppa, mow, mow" became a singalong moment at every gathering imaginable. The track proved that country artists could genuinely compete on the pop charts without compromising their sound.

Following that success, "Bobbie Sue" doubled down on the retro-pop formula that worked so well. Penned by Wood Newton and the married songwriting team of Dan and Adele Tyler, the 1982 track kicked off with a driving beat reminiscent of 1960s bubblegum pop, featuring blazing guitar work and the Muscle Shoals Horns. Joe Bonsall called it "a 1982 Elvira," and decades later, it remains an underrated gem that deserves more recognition in discussions of [country music](https://infeeds.com/tags/country) classics.

"American Made" arrived in 1983 as an unexpectedly prescient anthem. The song celebrated homegrown women while subtly critiquing the foreign goods flooding American markets. With its light touch and good-natured warmth, it never felt mean-spirited, scaling to Number One on the country charts and eventually becoming the title of their farewell tour.

## The Gospel Foundation Never Faded

While casual fans may recognize the Oaks primarily for their country hits, their spiritual roots remained fundamental to their identity. "Trying to Love Two Women" infused a cheating song with the kind of complex harmonies that showcased their technical prowess. Written by the great Sonny Throckmorton in 1980, the cautionary tale warned against spreading love too thin: "Trying to love two women is like a ball and chain."

Their second country Number One, "Leaving Louisiana (In the Broad Daylight)," was a Rodney Crowell composition that highlighted everything thrilling about the vocal group. The way they drew out the word "crazy" when singing about "crazy chances" demonstrated expert phrasing and storytelling that few groups could execute with such precision.

"Y'All Come Back Saloon" marked their first hit single in 1977, written by Sharon Vaughn as her debut songwriting effort. The sweet and smoky honky-tonk ballad contained just enough church-related references to appeal to the quartet's gospel roots, helping establish them during the era when story-songs dominated both country and pop radio.

## Evolution and Experimentation

The 2018 album "17th Avenue Revival," produced by Dave Cobb, found the group returning to their hymnbook stylings with remarkable results. "Brand New Star" became particularly poignant, celebrating those who had passed on with vigorous handclaps and joyous arrangements that balanced faith with genuine emotion.

Their willingness to experiment proved crucial to their longevity. Even in their later years, producer Dave Cobb pitched them an unconventional idea: covering a White Stripes staple. Initially skeptical, the band entered the studio and delivered something genuinely different, replacing Jack White's heavy guitar riffs with layered vocal harmonies that brought audiences to their feet.

The Oak Ridge Boys demonstrated that versatility and authenticity could coexist, that a group rooted in gospel traditions could dominate pop charts without losing their soul. That balance between spiritual conviction and commercial ambition remains their greatest achievement in [entertainment](https://infeeds.com/tags/entertainment) history.
