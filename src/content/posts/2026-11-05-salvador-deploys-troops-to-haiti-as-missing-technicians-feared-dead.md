Haiti's gang violence has reached a critical turning point. The disappearance of four Salvadoran aviation technicians two weeks ago has triggered a major military response that could reshape the Caribbean nation's security landscape, but experts worry the cure might be worse than the disease.

Salvadoran President Nayib Bukele wasted no time dispatching more than 2,000 troops and military hardware to Haiti after the October 18 kidnapping. His social media posts paint a picture of decisive action: videos of heavily armed soldiers patrolling Port-au-Prince streets, helicopters opening fire on gang positions, locals welcoming the foreign forces. "Gather your gangs so we can go fight with them," Bukele posted on X, a message that sounds more like a provocation than a humanitarian mission.

Haitian police recently discovered the burned-out truck used by the missing technicians in the Santo neighbourhood outside Port-au-Prince, discovered during a joint operation with UN-backed forces and Salvadoran troops. But finding the vehicle hasn't brought any closer to finding the men.

## Accountability questions loom large

Here's where things get complicated. Only about 200 of El Salvador's soldiers actually fall under the UN-backed Gang Suppression Force's jurisdiction, according to UN officials. That means roughly 1,800 troops are operating with murky oversight and unclear accountability measures.

UN spokesman Stephane Dujarric reminded everyone this week that "any security operation be done within the framework of international human rights law." It's a gentle warning wrapped in diplomatic language, but the subtext is clear: nobody is entirely sure who's in charge when bullets start flying.

Haiti's Prime Minister Alix Didier Fils-Aime insists the foreign forces operate under Haitian authority and support rather than replace the state. Yet the timing raises eyebrows. Haiti has been without an elected government since 2021, when President Jovenel Moise was assassinated. The Provisional Electoral Council just announced it's delaying national elections until July 2027, pushing them back from December.

## The civilian toll is already mounting

Doctors Without Borders painted a grim picture. In just eight days following the technicians' disappearance, 30 people including women and children were injured by heavy gunfire and drone explosions. The organization fears the escalating conflict will claim more victims and displace more people, particularly with additional international forces arriving.

This matters because Haiti is already in crisis. Some 5.94 million Haitians are facing acute hunger according to a recent food security assessment. Gang-controlled roads have made it nearly impossible to reach voter registration centers, with fewer than one million registered voters out of 6.5 million eligible Haitians. Violence has blocked civilians from essential services and made travel to healthcare facilities "extremely risky."

Even gang leaders are sending distress signals. Wilson Joseph, leader of the 400 Mawozo gang, told the Associated Press he'd written to the UN Secretary-General requesting dialogue, expressing concern about civilian casualties in the escalating fighting. Whether that's genuine remorse or tactical positioning remains unclear.

Bukele's tough-on-crime approach has worked at home, where he's jailed tens of thousands and maintained a state of emergency that rights groups say suspends civil rights. But exporting that model to Haiti's chaotic environment, where state institutions are fragile and gang networks are deeply entrenched, presents an entirely different challenge.

The fundamental question haunting Haiti's future is whether importing external security forces can actually stabilize a nation that desperately needs functioning institutions, not just firepower. Or will the presence of foreign troops with limited oversight simply create new problems while failing to solve the old ones?
