When Anduril founder Palmer Luckey announced a partnership with Qualcomm to build laptops entirely in the United States starting in 2027, it sounded like a manufacturing revolution. No major US computer company builds laptops domestically anymore. Not Apple, not Dell, not HP, not Microsoft. But within 48 hours of journalists asking pointed questions, Anduril's commitment shifted dramatically.

On Monday's call with reporters, Luckey was emphatic. He wanted to make literally every part of a computer in the United States and claimed Anduril had already mapped out a technical roadmap to achieve 100 percent domestic production over time. "We are not just barely meeting the bar for Made in America," he declared.

Then came the specifics. When asked directly what percentage of components would be made in the US by 2027, Luckey's answer was blunt: "TBD." He explained that critical decisions remained unsettled. Would memory fabrication facilities be online? CPU and GPU fabs? Could they secure a US display line? The prototype, he admitted, wasn't anywhere close to his stated goal.

## The Numbers Game

Anduril's initial response to follow-up questions was even more revealing. A company spokesperson confirmed the prototype currently sat at just 32 percent US-origin components by procurement cost. They wouldn't commit to hitting 65 percent by launch, instead vaguely promising to "expand domestic sourcing."

Then, one day later, everything changed. Anduril communications VP Shannon Prior announced the company was "indeed being developed to exceed the 65% U.S. domestic-content threshold at launch in 2027." This 65 percent figure is not arbitrary. It's the exact threshold established by the Biden Administration's updated Buy American Act, which governs what federal agencies can purchase without a waiver.

Luckey had actually highlighted this standard on the Monday call, arguing that government agencies were already circumventing domestic procurement laws through waivers en masse. If Anduril hits 65 percent, their laptop would theoretically qualify as American-made under federal standards.

## The Language Matters

But here's where the nuance gets critical. The original press release specified the laptop would be "designed and assembled" in the United States, not necessarily "manufactured." Assembly and manufacturing are vastly different. One involves putting existing components together; the other means actually producing those components domestically. This same distinction plagued discussions with router maker [TP-Link](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) just weeks earlier.

Luckey did outline some genuine domestic production plans. Anduril has lined up a US glassmaker for cover glass, is developing made-in-America batteries, and is programming FPGAs to replace power management chips normally sourced from China. The enclosure is designed for US machining using domestically producible metals.

Qualcomm's contribution involves manufacturing Snapdragon processors at TSMC's Arizona facility, which at least keeps fabrication on US soil, even if it's technically a Taiwanese company.

## Trust and Verification

The rapid reversal raises legitimate questions about transparency. Why did Anduril change its answer after scrutiny? Was the internal calculation always different from what leadership initially claimed? Or does this reflect genuine uncertainty about supply chain feasibility?

History suggests healthy skepticism is warranted. Companies have strategically assembled products domestically to appease political pressure, only to abandon the effort. Others have promised domestic jobs and delivered empty buildings. At least one company fabricated false claims about US manufacturing to government agencies.

Anduril's shift from "TBD" to "65 percent commitment" in one day suggests either the company wasn't transparent initially or didn't fully understand its own supply chain capabilities. Neither scenario inspires confidence, even if the final product does meet the Buy American threshold.

The 2027 launch gives Anduril time to make good on these promises, but the real test won't arrive until that laptop actually ships and auditors verify the claim. Until then, maybe the smarter question isn't whether Anduril can build a truly American computer, but why no one successfully has before.
