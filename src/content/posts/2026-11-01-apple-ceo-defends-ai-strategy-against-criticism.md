Apple's new CEO John Ternus recently sat down with Wired's Steven Levy to address a persistent criticism: that the company has fallen behind in the artificial intelligence race. It's a fair question, especially given Apple's delayed rollout of an enhanced Siri and years of headlines questioning whether the Cupertino giant was sleeping on AI.

But Ternus, who took the helm at Apple just last September after a long tenure as an executive, wasn't defensive. Instead, he offered a refreshingly different perspective on how Apple views AI and its role in the company's future products.

## The "AI for AI's Sake" Trap

"Everyone has an opinion," Ternus acknowledged during the interview. "I really like where we're at." It's a bold statement given the competitive landscape, but he elaborated with reasoning that cuts through the noise of the AI hype cycle.

Apple will absolutely leverage AI in its upcoming products, Ternus assured. The company isn't ignoring the technology or pretending it doesn't matter. However, the company's philosophy diverges sharply from competitors racing to inject AI into every feature imaginable. "We don't want to ship AI for AI's sake," he said.

This is the key difference. While others chase headlines with flashy AI announcements, Apple is focused on a simpler metric: does it actually make people happy? "When we're doing it right, people won't have to think about whether it was AI that made this happen or more traditional software. What matters is, did we build something that they really love?"

It's a consumer-centric argument that feels almost quaint in today's tech industry, where AI announcements dominate every earnings call and product launch.

## The Agent Question

When Levy pressed further, asking whether AI agents might fundamentally transform how people interact with technology, potentially making traditional apps obsolete, Ternus took a philosophical stance. Such developments "could make life really easy, I suppose," he replied, but he emphasized something often lost in tech discussions.

"The creation of products is a deeply human and personal thing, something that we love to do."

This statement reveals Apple's positioning: the company sees itself not just as a [technology](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) vendor, but as a creative enterprise. Reducing everything to AI-driven agents might optimize efficiency, but it potentially strips away the craftsmanship and intentionality that Apple has built its brand around.

## Timing and Trust

There's something strategic here beyond mere philosophy. Apple's delayed approach to AI actually builds credibility. Rather than rush features to market, the company is betting that when it does integrate AI meaningfully, users will trust it more. That's especially important for a company handling personal data across billions of devices.

The summer launch of the improved Siri, while late compared to competitors' offerings, signals Apple's willingness to take its time. Speed to market matters, but so does execution quality. Ternus seems confident that people will eventually see Apple's restraint as wisdom rather than hesitation.

Compare this to the broader [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) industry landscape, where every company from startups to mega-cap corporations is scrambling to prove their AI credentials. The noise is deafening. In that context, Apple's more measured approach stands out, whether you view it as thoughtful strategy or risk aversion.

## The Real Stakes

Ultimately, Ternus is making a bet about what people actually want from technology. Not flashy demos or cutting-edge capabilities for their own sake, but tools that genuinely improve daily life. It's a message Apple has successfully sold for years, and it might just work for AI too. The question isn't whether Apple will succeed with this philosophy, but whether consumers will reward a company that prioritizes substance over spectacle when every other player is shouting about their AI breakthroughs.

So the real question becomes: in an AI-obsessed market, can the company that says "we're not rushing this" ultimately prove it was right all along?
