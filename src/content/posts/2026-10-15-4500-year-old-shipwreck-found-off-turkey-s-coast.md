## An Ancient Discovery Beneath the Waves

Off the southern coast of Turkey, near Kekova Island, archaeologists have uncovered what could be the world's oldest shipwreck. Dating back approximately 4,500 years to between 2700 and 2400 BC, this Early Bronze Age wreck was first spotted during an underwater survey in 2024. The team, led by Hakan Öniz from Akdeniz University in Antalya, has since completed excavations that reveal a fascinating window into ancient Mediterranean trade.

The wooden hull of the vessel has long decomposed, but the cargo tells a compelling story. Archaeologists recovered around 30 amphoras, or ancient storage jars, from the seabed. These vessels display distinctive characteristics: side handles and flat bases typical of pottery from the Levant and Egypt during the Early Bronze Age. The shape itself offers clues about the ship's purpose and the era it sailed through.

## Trading Routes and Maritime Mystery

Oniz estimates the ship measured roughly 10 meters long and likely embarked from a harbor along the eastern Mediterranean coast. The crew would have sailed along the shoreline, collecting amphoras filled with olive oil or wine from different villages. Their destination remains somewhat mysterious, but the archaeologists believe they were headed toward either the Minoan civilization on Crete or the city of Troy in northwestern Turkey when disaster struck.

What's particularly intriguing is that the amphoras show minor variations between them, suggesting they originated from different workshops and settlements. This suggests a coordinated trading network existed 4,500 years ago, far more sophisticated than previously understood.

The site itself tells another story through additional artifacts. Several stone anchors were discovered some distance from the main wreck, a telltale sign they may have been jettisoned when the vessel encountered trouble. The shipwreck rests about 30 meters below the surface on a treacherous section of coast notorious for strong currents and violent winds. It's easy to imagine the final moments of this ancient crew caught in a Mediterranean storm.

## How This Compares to Other Discoveries

Turkey's Mediterranean coast has yielded hundreds of ancient shipwrecks over the years. Most date to the Roman and Byzantine periods, but a handful represent Bronze Age maritime endeavors. The famous Uluburun wreck, discovered in 1982, dates to around 1300 BC and contained treasures including ebony, ivory, and ostrich eggs from across the Mediterranean world.

Other Bronze Age wrecks exist along this coast too. Öniz himself has identified two additional Bronze Age shipwrecks in recent surveys, one from the Middle Bronze Age and another from the Early Bronze Age. However, none appear to be as old as Kekova. The previous record holder was the Early Bronze Age wreck at Dokos in southern Greece, dated to 2700-2200 BC.

The catch? Not everyone is ready to crown Kekova definitively the world's oldest shipwreck. Jon Henderson from the University of Edinburgh urges caution, noting that dating based on pottery comparisons can be imprecise since similar forms and decorative features sometimes appear across longer time periods. Radiocarbon data would provide stronger confirmation, but unfortunately no organic material has survived at this particular site.

## Why This Matters Beyond the Headlines

Henderson points out something crucial that gets lost in the rush to claim records: what truly captivates scientists isn't whether Kekova is a century or two older than Dokos. Rather, it's that we're glimpsing a snapshot of Mediterranean maritime trade more than 4,000 years ago. We know people were exchanging goods across the sea during this period, but finding physical remains of this activity remains extraordinarily rare.

This discovery challenges assumptions about how organized and sophisticated early trade networks were. It suggests commerce and coordination weren't spontaneous acts but carefully orchestrated ventures involving multiple settlements working together. The strategic choice to use flat-bottomed amphoras before traders eventually switched to tapered, carrot-shaped bottles reveals how practical concerns shaped commercial evolution.

We're left wondering what other stories lie waiting on the Mediterranean seafloor, and whether future discoveries might push back the timeline of maritime trade even further.
