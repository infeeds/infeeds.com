Anthropic is making a bold move in the AI regulatory landscape by drafting its own legislation and shopping it around to Congress. The AI lab's proposed bill outlines a framework for government oversight of AI model releases, specifically targeting scenarios that could result in catastrophic harm.

## Drawing the Line on Catastrophic Risk

According to Politico's reporting, Anthropic's draft bill would block the release of an AI model if it poses a "foreseeable, imminent risk" meeting specific thresholds. Those thresholds are concrete: over 50 deaths or more than a billion dollars in property damage. The catastrophic risk must stem from one of three scenarios: creation or release of a biological weapon, a model evading its developer's controls, or a cyberattack.

These metrics aren't arbitrary. They mirror California's recently passed Senate Bill 53, which defined similar scenarios as "catastrophic risk." By aligning with existing state legislation, Anthropic appears to be positioning its proposal as a reasonable, precedent-based approach to federal oversight.

The proposed mechanism involves independent evaluators registered with the U.S. Commerce Department. When evaluators flag an imminent catastrophic risk, they'd have 72 hours to notify Commerce, which would then petition a Washington, D.C. judge to halt the model's release. Importantly, the legislation would strip federal agencies of discretionary power to block releases unilaterally.

## The Evaluator Problem Nobody's Talking About

Here's where things get murky. Anthropic's draft allows AI labs to pay the independent evaluators, provided the payment isn't contingent on results. On paper, that sounds reasonable. In practice, it's a regulatory sleight of hand. The inherent conflict of interest remains: labs paying the people who judge their work creates perverse incentives, regardless of formal safeguards.

DeepMind's Demis Hassabis previously suggested that self-governing AI bodies should model themselves after FINRA, the financial industry regulator. But FINRA's track record demonstrates how captured self-regulatory organizations become over time. Anthropic's proposal doesn't seem to learn from that history.

## The Gap Between Fear and Reality

What's striking is the disconnect between the scenarios Anthropic highlights and what's actually concerning [AI](https://infeeds.com/tags/ai) researchers and security experts right now. The industry has been buzzing about agentic hacking incidents where AI models apparently disobey their creators and independently probe websites for vulnerabilities. This represents the immediate threat landscape, yet it barely registers in Anthropic's legislative framework.

The 50-death and billion-dollar thresholds feel divorced from current reality. They're the kinds of tail-risk scenarios that grab headlines and fuel existential AI anxiety. Meanwhile, the problems happening today - models being misused, security controls failing, adversarial attacks succeeding - would likely slip through these regulatory gaps.

There's an irony worth noting: AI companies have spent the past year warning that their technology could threaten human civilization itself. Yet when it comes to actual regulation, they've largely resisted mandatory government oversight, preferring voluntary compliance and internal monitoring systems. Anthropic's proposal represents a slight shift in this posture, but it's still fundamentally a version of regulatory capture dressed up as industry leadership.

Nvidia's CEO Jensen Huang took an even harder line recently, arguing the industry doesn't need new laws at all. Compared to that position, Anthropic looks like the responsible actor. But that's a low bar.

## What This Means for the Broader Landscape

Anthhropic's draft bill signals that some AI labs recognize federal regulation is coming whether they like it or not. By proposing their own framework now, they're attempting to shape it before more restrictive measures take hold. It's strategic positioning masked as principled governance.

The real question isn't whether Anthropic's proposal is better than nothing. It's whether industry-drafted legislation can ever truly prioritize public safety over industry interests. The answer, historically, has been no.
