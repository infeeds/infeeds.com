Games look absolutely gorgeous with ray tracing enabled, but the visual upgrade comes with a steep price tag. Not in dollars, but in frames per second.

The current console generation made ray tracing a mainstream feature, with the PS5 and Xbox Series X offering moderate ray-traced experiences. But PC gaming exists in a different stratosphere. High-end rigs can deliver the most advanced ray tracing with incredible fidelity, while average machines struggle just to keep up.

## The Hardware Reality

Here's the truth: if your GPU is more than five years old, don't even bother enabling ray tracing. The technology is a luxury feature reserved for those with fairly high-end rigs. A recent comprehensive analysis by TechSpot tested ray tracing across 36 different games and found that average frame rates dropped between 20 to 40 percent when RT was enabled. Some titles were absolute killers for performance.

Hogwarts Legacy saw a 45 percent frame rate dip. Alan Wake 2 dropped around 52 percent. But the worst offender? Hitman 3 experienced a brutal 65 percent decrease in average frame rates. That's a massive sacrifice for a lighting technique most players won't even notice while playing.

For anyone gaming on an RTX 3060 or AMD Radeon RX 6600 XT, ray tracing should stay disabled. Focus instead on achieving a stable frame rate at your monitor's native resolution. If you've never experienced ray tracing in action, you won't miss it.

## What Actually Is Ray Tracing?

Ray tracing is an advanced rendering technique that simulates realistic lighting effects, shadows, and reflections far more accurately than traditional rasterization methods. In older games without ray tracing support, screen-space reflections are used, which lack accuracy. Modern titles supporting RT bounce rays around the screen to reflect offscreen objects and calculate how the in-game world should actually be lit.

The visual improvement is real. But improving your [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) setup just to enable ray tracing? That's a different conversation entirely.

## The Path Tracing Problem

If ray tracing is a talented apprentice, path tracing is a full-blown master. This advanced variation of RT can calculate far more lighting sources than traditional ray tracing, leading to jaw-dropping results in the handful of games that support it.

Path tracing handles the entire look of a game using multi-bounce global illumination, rather than combining RT with traditional rasterization for specific effects. The differences are subtle but noticeable. In Resident Evil Requiem's opening scene, path tracing captures a slight red tinge cast by neon lights from an off-screen shop window onto a car's headlight. Standard ray tracing misses this entirely.

But here's the catch: path tracing is even more brutally demanding than traditional ray tracing. In Cyberpunk 2077, enabling path tracing can be 50 percent more demanding than regular RT depending on the scene. Unless you own an RTX 5090 or RTX 5080, path tracing should stay firmly off your settings menu.

## The Frame Generation Gamble

NVIDIA's DLSS 4.5 Multi Frame Generation technology can boost frame rates when ray tracing is enabled, but it comes with a trade-off: increased input lag. For competitive gamers, that's unacceptable. For single-player enthusiasts willing to embrace the tech, it's a viable workaround.

The Steam Hardware Survey shows the NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 leading in usage share, which suggests dedicated PC players are investing in [graphics cards](https://infeeds.com/tags/hardware) capable of handling ray tracing. These folks can enjoy the technology responsibly. But if your GPU is a couple of generations old, keep RT disabled and pocket those frame rates instead.

Ray tracing delivers genuine visual improvements that adoring the technology is easy when you have the hardware to back it up. The question isn't whether ray tracing looks better, but whether you're willing to sacrifice performance for beauty.
