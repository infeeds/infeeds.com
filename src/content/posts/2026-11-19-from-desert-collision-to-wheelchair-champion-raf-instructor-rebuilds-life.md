On 21 January 2016, Rob Bugden's life changed in an instant. The 30-year-old RAF parachute jumping instructor was executing what should have been a routine landing formation over the Arizona desert when his friend Brook Stebbings' knee collided with him at 1,000 feet. The impact shattered his neck, and Bugden plummeted to the sand, entangled with Stebbings like "a bag of washing above our heads," according to the ground team.

When he woke in a Phoenix hospital, groggy and disoriented, Bugden's first coherent thought wasn't about his own fate. It was: "What's my brother done?" His sibling had served across Northern Ireland, Sierra Leone, Kosovo, Bosnia, Iraq and Afghanistan - basically everywhere he'd been, he'd been shot at. But this time, it was the careful parachutist, not the soldier, who lay broken.

## A Military Life, Interrupted

Bugden came from military stock. Three grandparents in the air force, a father with 22 years in the Royal Navy, a brother still serving. "It's a bit like the family business," he'd say later. But he'd tried a different path first, studying sports coaching at Cardiff University and running long distance for Great Britain under 23s. In 2006, he was on the same British team as Mo Farah, though he admits with characteristic humour, "the less said about my race, the better."

When Olympic dreams faded, Bugden joined the RAF in 2008, eventually deciding to become a parachute jumping instructor despite a lifelong fear of heights. "I never got over it," he says, "but I think I faced it." The air force sent him to Blythe, Arizona, a trucker stop town on the California border, for high altitude jump training. His dream was to work with special forces on rescue operations like the Tristan da Cunha mission. That dream ended at 1,000 feet.

The injuries were catastrophic. Severe spinal cord damage left Bugden tetraplegic - all four limbs affected. He'd need round-the-clock care for life. For the first four months, he couldn't move at all. People fed him, brushed his teeth, washed his face. Beyond mobility, he faced three of the biggest complications of spinal injury: bladder, bowel, and sexual function difficulties.

## Measuring Recovery in Inches

It took 10 months in rehabilitation to regain even basic independence. Learning to manage his catheter. Standing with assistance. Feeling his 6ft 3in frame vertical again, even if only briefly. "It's quite nice being tall," he says.

Four years passed before Bugden was fully discharged from rehab. The psychological hurdles matched the physical ones. Recovery, he learned, isn't linear. "It's peaks and troughs," he explains. "You'll do really well, then you'll plateau, then you'll dip, then you'll come up a little bit and plateau. Sounds really weird, but I enjoy it. Because when I was lying in a bed, that was the worst time for me."

Today, Bugden lives in an adapted bungalow in rural Wales with Denzel, his "lack of assistance dog." His walls display England running vests and signed rugby shirts. His life looks remarkably similar to before, just reconfigured. "I'm still me," he says. "I can still chat bollocks to the best of them. I just have to do things differently now."

His friends Aaron and Mat, both present during the accident, still train military parachutists but keep Bugden's collision at the front of their minds. They don't walk on eggshells around him. They've literally pushed his wheelchair up hills when needed. After the accident, they launched Horus, a rugby team raising funds for injured veterans, which set a world record for the longest rugby 7s and 10s match, played continuously for over 24 hours.

In 2023, Bugden competed in five half-marathons across Wales in a custom sports wheelchair, raising £8,000 for charities that saved his life. He's now an ambassador for multiple veteran support organisations including the RAF Benevolent Fund and Help for Heroes.

He quotes Al Pacino's Coach D'Amato from Any Given Sunday: "We can climb out of hell one inch at a time." Then he adds his own philosophy: "Inches make metres, metres make miles, and miles make a lifetime."

It's not always easy. But Bugden has learned that recovery, like life itself, isn't measured in dramatic leaps - it's measured in the accumulation of small, persistent choices.
