Getting up from a chair might seem trivial, but it's actually one of the most revealing indicators of your overall [health](https://infeeds.com/tags/health) and longevity. The sit-to-stand chair test, also called chair rises, is a deceptively simple 30-second evaluation that measures strength, mobility and power. Experts increasingly rely on it to assess functional capacity in patients of all ages.

"Getting up from a chair may seem like a very simple task, but it actually requires several systems to work together," explains Joseph Hribick, a clinical assistant professor of physical therapy at Lebanon Valley College in Pennsylvania. "You need adequate strength and power in the muscles of your hips and legs, good balance, coordination, joint mobility and enough cardiovascular capacity to repeat this movement several times."

## Why This Test Matters More Than You'd Think

The reason this test is so valuable is deceptively straightforward: it's functional, inexpensive, and backed by extensive research. When you perform a chair rise, your body coordinates motion through your hips, knees, ankles, trunk and balance system while moving your own body weight through space. It's something we do dozens of times daily, which means the results have real-world relevance.

"The chair stand isn't a crystal ball for longevity, but it is a simple window into our functional reserve, alongside things like walking speed, balance and grip strength," says Hazel Anderson, an associate professor at the University of St. Augustine for Health Sciences. The goal isn't necessarily a perfect score. It's maintaining the strength and movement needed to keep doing the things you love.

Muscle power declines significantly as we age. After 40, people typically lose 3 to 5 percent of muscle mass annually unless they actively work to prevent it. This loss cascades into decreased mobility, increased fall risk, and ultimately, loss of independence. Research involving over 11,000 older adults has shown that insufficient sit-to-stand muscle power is directly associated with mobility limitations and disability in daily activities.

## The Cognitive Connection

Here's something surprising: older adults who passed the chair-rise test also demonstrated strong cognitive abilities. Standing up quickly requires motor planning, coordination, attention, and rapid sensory integration. Those who maintain physical activity and better neuromuscular function often preserve cognitive health as well, creating a virtuous cycle of wellness.

According to CDC guidelines, a 60-year-old man should complete at least 14 chair rises in 30 seconds, while a woman should achieve at least 12. If you fall short of these benchmarks, additional fall risk assessments may be warranted.

## How to Perform the Test and Build Strength

The procedure is straightforward. Place a sturdy chair against a wall, sit with feet flat and arms crossed over your chest. Stand up by extending your hips and knees, then sit back down without using your arms. The movement should remain controlled enough that you can immediately stand again upon contact with the seat.

If you're training (rather than testing), it's perfectly fine to use your hands, a higher chair, or support from someone nearby. Anderson suggests aiming for 8 to 12 controlled reps, one to three times daily. "The goal with everyday training isn't to race the clock," she explains. "I'd much rather see safe, controlled and appropriately challenging repetitions."

To increase difficulty, try rising from a lower surface, adding more reps, or holding a weight. The key is consistency and proper form, not speed.

If you're struggling to stand without pushing off armrests or rocking, consult your healthcare provider. This could signal declining lower-extremity function, especially if accompanied by falls, dizziness, pain or weakness.

## The Real Goal

Dr. Jennifer Timmons, a physician specializing in longevity, emphasizes treating the chair stand test as a benchmark, not an endpoint. "Don't train simply to pass the chair stand test. Train so that being able to stand up from a chair or a toilet remains effortless, so that you can maintain independence as you age."

The encouraging news: strength and power remain trainable well into older adulthood. This test isn't about achieving perfection; it's about understanding what your body can do right now and committing to maintaining or improving it. Because ultimately, independence isn't measured by a [fitness](https://infeeds.com/tags/fitness) score, but by your ability to live life on your own terms.
