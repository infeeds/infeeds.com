Democrats are now scrutinizing how the Trump administration handled Nvidia's chip sales to China, particularly after the company's founder Jensen Huang was ousted following his admission of an extramarital affair with a woman allegedly connected to Chinese intelligence.

The inquiry, led by Rep. Richard Neal (D-Mass.) and Sen. Ron Wyden (D-Ore.), represents the second Congressional investigation into the matter since news of the alleged honeypot scandal broke. Their letter to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick raises serious questions about whether national security was compromised in pursuit of corporate interests and potential personal leverage.

## The Questionable Arrangement

Here's how the scheme worked, according to the Democrats' letter: Trump agreed to allow Nvidia to sell its H200 chips to China on one condition. The chips, manufactured by Taiwan, had to be shipped to the United States first. Upon arrival, Trump slapped on a tariff, essentially taking a cut of Nvidia's China sales through what Democrats argue is an unconstitutional export tax system. The Commerce Department then issued an export license to proceed.

The revenue-sharing deal itself wasn't hidden. In August 2025, both Nvidia and AMD publicly agreed to surrender 15% of their revenue from Chinese chip sales to the U.S. government in exchange for export licenses. But the Democrats' concern cuts deeper than a simple tariff arrangement.

Their letter questions why the Commerce Department continued approving purchases by Megaspeed, a Singapore-based Nvidia customer, even after U.S. intelligence reportedly flagged concerns about the company. More troubling: the alleged spy, Alice Huang, was an executive at Megaspeed and had a relationship with Jensen Huang. It's a tangled web that suggests either gross negligence or deliberate oversight.

## The Leverage Question

What really stings in this investigation is the timing and the donations. Nvidia contributed $26 million toward Trump's projects, including his ballroom, after Trump learned about Huang's "compromised position." The Democrats are essentially asking: did Trump use knowledge of the affair to extract donations from Nvidia?

That question gets sharper when you consider Trump's own comments in a recent "60 Minutes" interview. He mentioned threatening an unnamed AI executive with jail time, and said the executive "totally changed" after the threat. While Trump didn't name names, the implication is hard to miss.

## The Broader Security Concern

Beyond the personal scandal and financial arrangements, there's a fundamental national security issue here. The Commerce Department apparently approved other Asian companies to purchase Nvidia chips despite those companies having "no physical presence at their listed addresses." This suggests a possible shell company operation designed to funnel advanced semiconductors to China, the very threat that export controls are meant to prevent.

[Waymo robotaxis](https://infeeds.com/tags/waymo) have faced criticism for regulatory violations, but Nvidia's potential role in circumventing national security protocols is on another level entirely. This isn't about a software glitch; it's about whether America's most critical technology is being routed to its greatest geopolitical competitor.

Huang agreed to testify before the Republican-led committees in a closed-door session after his resignation, but his departure doesn't resolve the broader questions about how this arrangement happened in the first place. Was the Commerce Department asleep at the wheel? Did political considerations override security concerns? And what does this say about how export controls actually work when they're administered by an administration with its own financial interests at stake?

The investigation will likely reveal whether this was a case of spectacular mismanagement or something far more calculated. Either way, it exposes troubling vulnerabilities in how we protect [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) assets that are vital to national security.
