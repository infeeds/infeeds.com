In the aftermath of recent midterm elections, far-right extremist groups are celebrating what they view as validation of their long-term strategy: infiltrating and ultimately controlling the Republican Party from the inside.

White nationalist Nick Fuentes kicked things off with a peculiar move on Election Day, urging his followers to vote Democratic. When the results came in, Fuentes claimed credit for the outcome on Telegram, dubbing it "the Groyper Effect" after his supporter base. The move signals a darker calculation among extremist leaders: that a Democratic administration will radicalize enough voters to eventually push them toward far-right ideology.

"Let the Democrats rule, see how that goes," Fuentes said during a livestream. "There will come a time when the far left will terrorize people, and they're going to push all of them into the arms of the right."

## The Numbers Tell a Story

But this isn't just talk. The Institute for Research and Education on Human Rights tracked 535 candidates running in statewide races who were members of at least one far-right Facebook group. Of the 476 races called by Friday, 338 of those candidates won, representing a 71 percent win rate. In Florida, Marshall Rawson, a Charlottesville Unite the Right attendee, easily defeated his Democratic opponent in a State House race.

These aren't fringe victories either. The vast majority of wins came from GOP incumbents retaining seats, meaning extremist-aligned candidates are already embedded in state legislatures across the country. Meanwhile, ballot measures on transgender restrictions, voter ID laws, and anti-immigrant policies passed in multiple states, giving extremist groups concrete wins to celebrate.

The Proud Boys framed these results as validation. "The ultimate rebuke of the current Republican establishment was citizens voting them out of office then doing their job for them by passing ballot measures," the Cape Fear Proud Boys wrote in their Telegram channel.

## Inside Game, Outside Options

Researchers tracking these movements note that extremist groups are interpreting the results through a dual lens. Yes, some of their candidates lost, but they're reading the 41-47 percent vote share in Democratic-won areas as proof that a substantial mass base remains ready for radicalization.

"Now is the time, if you're a nationalist, to start looking at moving on the Republican Party where you live," wrote Warren Balogh, a white supremacist from Pittsburgh, calling for "a hostile takeover." The Cape Fear Proud Boys were more blunt: "You're going to run people we like and you're going to do what we say."

Not everyone is willing to wait for the next election cycle. Christian nationalist pastors, including conspiracy theorist Alex Jones, are meeting at a [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech)-enabled conference in Texas where attendees plan to discuss "battle plans" for seizing power. Some figures, like pardoned January 6 rioter Jake Lang, are already organizing events around congressional certification dates.

Experts haven't documented explicit calls for violence yet, but the rhetoric has shifted from vague threats to specific tactical discussions about infiltration and institutional capture. The strategy is becoming clearer: extremist groups see the GOP as vulnerable, riddled with what they perceive as insufficiently committed leadership. They believe they can either force the party to their specifications or replace it with one that will.

The disturbing part isn't the grandiose rhetoric. It's that their math checks out. With hundreds of extremist-aligned candidates winning seats and state ballot measures passing that advance their agenda, they have tangible proof their approach is working. Whether that base is as mobilizable as they claim remains to be seen, but the infrastructure for [political extremism](https://infeeds.com/tags/extremism) is being built in real time, hidden within the machinery of an established political party.
