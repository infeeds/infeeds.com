Leon Botstein's half-century tenure at Bard College came to an abrupt end this week, but not before raising serious questions about institutional accountability and power. After months of scrutiny following his initial announcement of stepping down as president in spring, Botstein resigned from all remaining roles at the liberal arts institution following damning allegations from the House Judiciary Committee.

## The Epstein Connection

The turning point came when Representatives Jamie Raskin and Madeleine Dean sent a letter to Bard's leadership detailing allegations that Botstein "likely enabled and possibly even participated in Epstein's abuse while serving as Bard's President." What made their case particularly compelling was a specific piece of evidence: a May 2014 email from Botstein's presidential account asking Jeffrey Epstein if a woman Epstein was allegedly grooming was "for me, or are you keeping her for yourself."

Raskin didn't mince words in his assessment. "It is hard to construct any kind of innocent explanation for Botstein's question and the surrounding events," he wrote, emphasizing that this exchange was directed to "the most famous sexual predator of our time about a woman Epstein was trafficking."

## A Pattern of Misconduct

The allegations against Botstein extend far beyond his connection to Epstein. According to the House Judiciary Committee's investigation, problematic behavior festered across campus for decades. The committee received multiple reports that Botstein personally engaged in sexual misconduct, from groping women to asking inappropriate sexualized questions during tenure interviews.

Perhaps most troubling is the revelation that Botstein personally oversaw Title IX cases in what the committee describes as a "highly irregular fashion." This essentially gave one man unchecked authority over sexual misconduct investigations at the institution. While Bard's Board Chair Brandon Weber claimed the college hasn't allowed the president unilateral authority over Title IX matters since 2015-2016, the damage from years of unchecked power raises questions about how many cases may have been mishandled.

## The Resignation and Denial

In his statement, Botstein maintained his innocence, claiming that "any assertion that I knew of or was involved in Epstein's criminal behavior is wrong." He framed his resignation as a sacrifice to protect Bard from distraction, stating he didn't want "false allegations" to put the institution at risk. Yet this explanation rings hollow given the specificity of the email evidence and the breadth of allegations spanning decades.

Bard College's leadership acknowledged they're conducting a full review of the allegations. Weber stated the institution is cooperating with the House Judiciary Committee's investigation, but such statements feel like damage control after the fact.

## What's Next

Raskin and Dean have also escalated matters by sending correspondence to Attorney General Todd Blanche and FBI Director Kash Patel, urging them to "undertake a serious investigation, rather than a cover-up, of the countless potential crimes suggested in evidence in the Epstein files."

This situation underscores a troubling [entertainment](https://infeeds.com/tags/entertainment) industry phenomenon: powerful figures insulated by their status and position, allowed to operate with minimal oversight for decades. Botstein wasn't immediately removed after initial Epstein revelations in spring. He continued teaching, retained prominent roles, and even lived in the official presidential residence while renovations were prepared for his off-campus home.

The pattern suggests institutional negligence at multiple levels. How many people at Bard knew something was amiss? How many complaints were quietly buried? And most critically, how many students were potentially impacted while administration prioritized protecting its figurehead?

Bard College's response will reveal whether it's genuinely committed to accountability or simply managing a PR crisis. The resignations are a start, but thorough investigations and systemic reforms are what's needed. When powerful academic leaders can operate unchecked for fifty years, it's not just one person's failure but an institutional breakdown.
