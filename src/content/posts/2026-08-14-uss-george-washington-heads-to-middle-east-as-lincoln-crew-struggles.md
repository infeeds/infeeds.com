The U.S. Navy is shifting its carrier presence in a significant move that underscores growing strain on personnel and equipment. The USS George Washington, based in the Pacific, has begun its journey toward the Middle East to relieve the USS Abraham Lincoln, which has been deployed for a record-breaking 260-plus days supporting operations against Iran.

The decision highlights mounting concerns about the human and mechanical toll of extended military operations. The Lincoln's crew has faced documented shortages of mail, food, and hygiene supplies as combat disrupted traditional supply routes. Reports of mental health struggles among sailors have also surfaced, though Navy officials have disputed the severity of conditions aboard.

## Stretched Too Thin at Sea

The Lincoln deployed from San Diego in November and arrived in the Middle East in January, just before hostilities with Iran escalated on February 28. What was originally planned as a standard deployment has stretched far beyond expectations. Navy leaders initially called reports of food shortages "false," only to later concede that supply disruptions occurred due to "combat actions" in the region.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth acknowledged the Lincoln's sailors deserve respect but pushed back against what he called "misrepresented" accounts of conditions. Yet Democratic lawmakers aren't satisfied. Arizona Senator Ruben Gallego, a Marine Corps veteran, called the situation "not just disgusting; it's dangerous" and plans a bipartisan oversight visit to the carrier.

The Lincoln originally was set to return home in May. Now, with the George Washington replacing it, the Navy faces questions about how long both carriers will remain deployed and what this means for personnel already stretched thin.

## Pacific Gets Left Empty

One under-discussed consequence of this maneuver involves the Pacific theater itself. Redeploying the George Washington to the Middle East leaves U.S. [news](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) forces without a carrier presence in Pacific waters for an undetermined period. The Trump administration views China as "a settled force" requiring deterrence but not the urgent threat designation it held under Biden.

This calculation may prove risky given China's military growth and regional assertiveness. Whether maintaining indefinite blockades of Iranian ports justifies leaving the Pacific unprotected remains an open debate within defense circles.

The George Washington left Vietnamese port in Da Nang last week and has been spotted crossing the Singapore Strait. The carrier strike group now heads through the Strait of Malacca, positioning itself for Indian Ocean transit and eventual Middle East station.

## A Pattern of Overextension

The Lincoln's struggles aren't isolated. The USS Gerald R. Ford just returned in May after an 11-month deployment, the longest since Vietnam. During that mission, a laundry fire forced repairs and left hundreds of sailors without sleeping quarters. The USS Harry S. Truman faced similar extended deployments while battling Houthi rebels in 2025.

A Navy official confirmed that a Lincoln sailor went overboard in early August but was quickly recovered and transferred for follow-up care. The Navy declined to specify whether this was considered a suicide attempt, citing privacy concerns. The agency also stated it hasn't observed increased suicidal ideation aboard but provided no supporting data.

These incidents reveal a systemic problem: the Navy is operating carriers and crews at unsustainable tempos. When sailors face 260 days at sea with disrupted supply chains and limited mental health resources, burnout becomes inevitable.

## What Comes Next

Hegseth indicated the Pentagon can maintain the Iranian blockade "indefinitely," yet offered no clarity on long-term strategy. The Trump administration hasn't articulated how or when the conflict might wind down. This uncertainty leaves sailors uncertain about their futures and families uncertain about when their loved ones will return.

Central Command defended sailor morale on social media, touting 10,000 aircraft flights and 1.5 million pounds of ordnance expended. Yet statistics about sorties and munitions tell us nothing about crew welfare, mental health, or whether current operational demands are sustainable.

The Navy must reconcile its strategic ambitions in the Middle East with the human cost of achieving them. Deploying fresh crews helps, but without addressing the underlying tempo of operations, the same problems will recur with the George Washington and future carriers.
