At TechBBQ in Copenhagen, the conversation among investors, founders, and operators from across Europe wasn't really about what to build with AI anymore. It was about who gets to control it.

No matter where you stood at the annual Nordic conference, from keynote stages to cocktail hours and after-parties, the same question kept surfacing: How can Europe gain more control over the technology powering AI? That question of sovereignty felt especially urgent after Anthropic's AI models Mythos and Fable became unavailable to users outside Europe earlier this year, disrupting startups and forcing the ecosystem to confront an uncomfortable reality.

## The Wake-Up Call

One startup exec told me the incident seriously disrupted his software team. Another shrugged it off, reasoning that while relying on two geopolitical powers for AI infrastructure might cause Europe severe headaches someday, everything is still functioning fine for now. But that complacency masks a deeper anxiety running through the room.

"TechBBQ's theme of agency felt particularly timely this year as the conversation shifted from what AI can do to what we're actually willing to let it do," Ellen de Brever, an angel investor and head of partnerships at the Novo Nordisk Foundation Cellerator, told me during the conference that wrapped on August 27.

The debate wasn't about smarter machines. It was about human judgment and who stays in the driver's seat. "The age of AI agents isn't really a story about machines gaining agency," de Brever continued. "It's a story about humans deciding what to give up."

## Privacy, Power, and Who Decides

One of the most refreshing sessions featured Signal President Meredith Whittaker, discussing whether privacy and [AI](https://infeeds.com/tags/ai) can coexist. She didn't mince words. Whittaker spoke out against AI assistants being integrated into operating systems, calling the current era a "data collection apparatus" powered by clever marketing that makes people forget about the collateral consequences.

"There is still a huge market need for privacy," she said. "And particularly with the sovereignty concerns, we will have customers here."

Emad Mostaque, co-founder of Stability AI, took a harder line. "Sovereignty is the ability to resist power being exerted over you," he argued during a panel about how an agentic workforce will reshape economics. He spoke bluntly about power concentration in the hands of a few AI labs and suggested that inevitably, "the person that controls the AI controls the country."

That comment landed hard in a room full of people who understand exactly what that means for European independence.

## Rethinking Everything

Mia Negru, advocacy and engagement director for the nonprofit Life With Artificials, captured the deeper stakes. "If intelligent agents perform cognitive work and robots increasingly perform physical work, we may be approaching something much bigger than another technological revolution," she told me.

"We may have to rethink ownership, work, democracy, economic participation and eventually even who gets to participate in society."

It's not just abstract philosophizing either. The stakes are real. When Anthropic restricted access to those models, it wasn't a technical glitch or a temporary hiccup. It was a reminder that European companies, no matter how sophisticated, remain dependent on decisions made in Silicon Valley and Beijing.

## The Human Factor

But conferences aren't all existential hand-wringing. There were rooftop barbecues hosted by Lovable, Nvidia, and OpenAI where founders chatted about Denmark's growing tech hubs. Ada Ventures gathered people at a cocktail bar to discuss the booming women's brain health space. And there was a speakers' dinner on an island in Copenhagen where the international press watched fire dancers while contemplating the future.

"In a room full of conversations about machines, the most memorable moments still come from the simplest thing," de Brever reflected. "Human connection, face-to-face, building relationships, sharing ideas, and reminding each other of what technology can never quite replace."

That tension, between the urgent need for European AI sovereignty and the very human desire to collaborate and connect, might be the real story emerging from Copenhagen. Europe's tech leaders understand they need to build differently. The question is whether they can actually pull it off.
