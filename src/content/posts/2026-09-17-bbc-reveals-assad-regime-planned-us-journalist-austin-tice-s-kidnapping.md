A years-long BBC investigation has exposed shocking details about the 2012 disappearance of US journalist Austin Tice, revealing that his kidnapping was not a chance encounter but a carefully orchestrated operation approved by Syrian President Bashar al-Assad himself.

For over a decade, the case of Tice, a Georgetown University law student and former US Marines captain, has remained one of the most significant missing-person cases in modern history. But new evidence uncovered by the BBC's podcast series "Where is Austin Tice? Series 3 of I'm Not A Monster Presents" paints a chilling picture of betrayal, surveillance, and calculated state planning.

## How the Operation Unfolded

Tice disappeared on August 14, 2012, while reporting on Syria's brutal civil war. He had been pushing into dangerous rebel-held territories to write front-page stories for the Washington Post, highlighting the Assad regime's documented human rights abuses. According to sources interviewed by the BBC, the kidnapping operation began when Tice's trusted driver, identified as Hamid Abu Obeid, informed regime contacts that the journalist was moving through opposition areas.

The driver passed this information to Sakr Rustom, an NDF militia member and nephew of Bassam al-Hassan, one of Assad's most trusted security figures and head of the National Defence Forces. Critically, Abu Ali, a former NDF member who spoke to the BBC, states that Hassan then telephoned President Assad directly to seek approval for the abduction.

"Abu Ali says he was subsequently called into Hassan's office and told the president had approved the operation," the investigation reveals.

## The Setup and the Betrayal

Before capturing Tice, the regime didn't act rashly. The driver suggested continuing surveillance to gather more intelligence. During this period, the driver made a brazen move: while Tice was interviewing rebel fighters, he secretly took the journalist's backpack containing his MacBook, satellite transmitter, and personal items for regime operatives to examine.

Tice's actions as a trained marine, including sharing military experience with fighters and correcting their weapon usage, had raised suspicion among those watching him. To regime operatives with limited understanding of foreign journalism, his behavior appeared suspicious, making him look less like a correspondent and more like a CIA operative. The BBC found no evidence supporting this claim, and a former senior US official has denied Tice held any intelligence role.

On August 11, 2012, Tice celebrated his 31st birthday at a rebel safehouse near Assad's presidential palace, complete with swimming pool, Free Syrian Army fighters, and Taylor Swift music. Days later, believing he was heading to Lebanon for a break, he was instead driven to Tahouneh, an area on Damascus's outskirts where Hassan maintained a state facility office.

What happened next represents a moment of devastating realization. According to Abu Ali's account, when the driver pulled up to the gates, he drew a gun. Tice laughed, thinking it was a joke. When the driver insisted he was serious, Tice realized his betrayal. In desperation, he pulled out his chequebook and pleaded with the driver to name his price rather than proceed with the kidnapping.

## The Aftermath and Unanswered Questions

Regime operatives covered Tice's eyes and took him into custody. Hassan immediately called President Assad to confirm they had captured the American. For years, the identity of the driver remained a mystery until BBC journalists traced the connection to Hamid Abu Obeid, a Palestinian refugee from Gaza living in poverty in Homs.

According to Abu Obeid's family, he had been promised Syrian citizenship, a passport, a house, and weapons in exchange for delivering Tice. After the abduction, he received only a pistol. When Assad's regime collapsed in December 2024, Abu Obeid disappeared under mysterious circumstances. His wife suspects he was either killed, detained, or forced into hiding.

Meanwhile, the fate of Austin Tice remains unknown. Secret Syrian intelligence files discovered in the regime's archives provided definitive proof that the state had held the journalist. Some former Syrian commanders claim he was executed, yet no remains have been found. His mother, Debra, continues pressing successive US presidents to investigate further, just as Abu Obeid's wife searches for her own missing husband.

Two families, separated by circumstance and geography, now share the same anguished quest: finding answers about loved ones lost to a regime's calculated cruelty.
