When Adreanna Pulliam downloaded the Christian dating app Upward last summer, she expected to find devout believers. Instead, she encountered moderate libertarians, MAGA types, and men whose profile photos featured them standing in front of funeral posters. The opera singer from Indiana realized quickly that something had shifted at Upward, one of Match Group's lesser-known [dating platforms](https://infeeds.com/tags/dating).

That shift became official last month when Upward relaunched with a new mission: stop being exclusively for Christians and start courting anyone interested in "traditional values" around family and faith, regardless of actual religious conviction. The rebrand was built on two data points from Match Group's 2026 Singles in America study: 72 percent of Gen Z want to get married, and a quarter of young singles identify with traditional relationship values.

But here's the problem. That survey claim masks a messier reality.

## The Marriage Myth Gen Z is Supposedly Embracing

Daniel Cox, author of "Generation Uncoupled: America's New Gender Divide and the Crisis of Human Connection," questions Match's interpretation of its own data. Gen Z is younger, he points out, which means more have never married yet. Comparing their responses to older generations who may already be divorced creates a misleading narrative.

The broader trends tell a different story. A 2023 Pew Research survey found that only 23 percent of adults consider marriage "extremely" or "very important" to living a fulfilling life, compared with 61 percent who prioritize close friendships. High school seniors' likelihood of marriage dropped from 80 percent in 1993 to 67 percent in 2023. Young women are driving this decline, increasingly believing that marriage would actually make them less happy.

"There's increasingly a belief among young women that marriage makes them less happy," Cox says. "It's not only that they have less desire to get married. There's a concern that if they get married, they won't be as happy as if they are single."

## The Trad Script as Cultural Band-Aid

Upward's new branding leans hard into nostalgia. The company's "Wanted: For Life" campaign features cowboy hats, men carrying women through meadows, and "chivalrymaxxing" overlays. It's Americana aesthetics meets courtship clarity.

Cox sees this trend as a reaction to cultural vacuum. Young people are desperate for *any* script around dating and relationships. As nontraditional approaches like polyamory and relationship anarchy gain traction, the traditional framework offers something comforting: defined roles and clear direction.

"People are wanting something over nothing," Cox explains. "The trad script is very clear. Though I don't think it's close to ideal for a lot of young people."

The irony is that Upward's own features contradict its conservative rebranding. Users can list body types, mark themselves as "it's complicated," skip Christian faith statements entirely, and even access options for open relationships. Married people can join. One user, Tauti, a social work student in California, lasted 48 hours before deleting the app, disturbed by the conservative majority seeking domesticated partners. She posted on TikTok that being Christian and liberal was "a big ask" on a supposedly faith-first platform.

## When Mission Drift Alienates Everyone

Upward faces an impossible bind. The app originally struggled with functionality issues, poor filtering, and user support complaints since its 2020 debut. Now it's trying to expand its user base by diluting what made it distinctive, alienating both progressive Christians and the conservative base that joined specifically for faith-focused matching.

Match Group won't disclose active user numbers. That silence speaks volumes.

Pulliam, despite wanting marriage eventually, doesn't consider it urgent. She recognizes Upward might find success "within communities of Christian private universities," but she's skeptical. "The only way that the app works is for maybe someone who has a little bit more of a closed mind," she says.

The real question isn't whether Gen Z wants marriage. It's whether they want marriage enough to compromise on the [things](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) that actually matter to their individual fulfillment. For many young women, the data suggests the answer remains no.
