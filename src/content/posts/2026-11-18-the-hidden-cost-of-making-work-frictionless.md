An employee has a question. A few years ago, they might have walked to a colleague's desk or waited for the next team meeting. Today, they can ask AI and get an answer in seconds.

That is progress. Good technology removes friction, reduces waiting and helps people move forward. The logic is irresistible: why interrupt someone when you can get the same answer instantly?

But what if that interruption was doing more than delivering information?

## The Hidden Value in Workplace Conversations

BambooHR's forthcoming research reveals something worth pausing over. Employees now spend nearly an hour and a half each day interacting with AI. Among the time people spend in actual conversation rather than working solo, about 2 in 5 interactions are now with AI instead of another person.

The reasons are obvious. AI is fast. It's always available. It doesn't make you feel stupid for asking. You can rephrase the same question three different ways without anyone's patience wearing thin.

Many employees prefer asking AI quietly rather than admitting they need help. I understand that. Asking another person carries social risk, and AI eliminates it almost entirely.

But here's what keeps nagging: that old conversation might have shown the employee how someone more experienced approached the problem. It might have surfaced context that was never written down. Sometimes a small interaction becomes a relationship that matters much later. From the perspective of the task, the interaction looked inefficient. From the perspective of the organization, it was building something.

## When Speed Isn't Everything

The BambooHR data points to a meaningful difference between time spent with AI and time spent with coworkers. AI time concentrates on completing tasks, experimenting, refining prompts and correcting output. Coworker time includes execution too, but much more of it flows toward sharing knowledge, brainstorming, mentoring and building relationships.

Those activities are harder to measure because they don't always produce an immediate deliverable. Their value shows up later, when a junior employee knows whom to call during a difficult moment. Or when someone watched an experienced colleague make a judgment call where the answer wasn't obvious.

This matters especially for people early in their careers. Most employees say they've learned something valuable from colleagues at different career stages. Many also believe that cross-generational mentorship is becoming harder as younger workers rely more heavily on AI.

## What Gets Lost in Translation

AI can give you a good answer. It might even explain it better than a rushed colleague. But professional judgment is rarely just the answer. It's knowing when the technically correct answer won't work in this company. Understanding which trade-off matters most. Recognizing a pattern because you've seen some version of the problem before.

A lot of that is learned by watching other people think. When we automate away the human interaction before understanding what it was actually doing inside the organization, we might be trading long-term capability for short-term efficiency.

The real question isn't whether AI should do certain tasks. The real question is what people should be freer to do because of it. The default answer in most organizations is more work. A task that took an hour now takes 20 minutes, so the remaining time fills up with another task, another message, another expectation. The efficiency disappears into a fuller calendar.

If AI gives a manager back an hour, maybe some of that should go toward coaching. If it gives an experienced employee more capacity, maybe there's more room to explain how they reached a decision rather than just sending the answer. That's work too. And I suspect it becomes more valuable as transactional work gets easier.

Some friction absolutely should disappear. Poor systems and unnecessary interruptions aren't worth preserving simply because they create human interaction. The opportunity is being more deliberate about what fills the capacity AI creates.

The more interesting question isn't what AI should do. It's what we do with the freedom it creates, and whether we'll use it to build organizations that are actually smarter over time.
