Delegates at the UN COP31 climate summit in Antalya are back at the negotiating table Sunday morning, wrestling with a third draft agreement that attempts to bridge deep divides between nations over fossil fuels and climate funding.

The new text, published in the early hours of Sunday by Turkish and Australian co-hosts, marks another attempt to salvage talks that have already blown past Friday's original deadline. While the draft still lacks explicit language calling for a "transition away from fossil fuels," it does acknowledge a commitment countries made three years ago to do exactly that. It's a compromise that satisfies no one completely, but may be just enough to keep talks from collapsing entirely.

## The Fossil Fuel Standoff

For the first time, the agreement recognizes that the world is on track to miss the crucial 1.5 degrees Celsius warming target set by the Paris climate accord. That's significant acknowledgment, but it also underscores the fundamental problem: countries keep agreeing the goal matters while failing to take decisive action.

The EU, Pacific island nations, and African states have been pushing hard to keep strong fossil fuel language in the text. Panama's Environment Minister Juan Carlos Navarro was blunt about the stakes: "It is not acceptable to have a COP or a cover decision that does not even mention fossil fuels and we simply will not put up with it." For low-lying Pacific nations like Tuvalu, it's literally a question of survival. Their climate ministers have called fossil fuel commitments a "red line."

On the other side, Saudi Arabia and other fossil fuel producers have fought tooth and nail to strip away any explicit references to coal, oil, and gas. Australia's Climate Change Minister Chris Bowen has argued the summit should focus on the shift toward clean energy rather than dwelling on what needs to end. That's a convenient framing when your country profits from the status quo.

## The Money Problem Nobody Wants to Talk About

Almost as contentious as the fossil fuel debate is the question of climate finance. Developing nations, particularly those in Africa, came to these talks expecting concrete commitments on adaptation funding. Earlier drafts included a figure of $120 billion annually by 2035. That's now gone, kicked to future meetings with no guarantees.

Ghana's Environment Minister Zanetor Agyeman Rawlings captured the frustration perfectly: "We did not come here to beg, we came here to claim what is rightfully ours for our people who are suffering the brunt of anthropogenic activities of developed countries." It's a fair point that rarely gets heard in polite diplomatic circles. Rich nations created the climate crisis. Poorer nations face the worst consequences and the fewest resources to adapt.

## What Happens If There's No Deal?

If countries cannot reach consensus by Sunday morning's reconvening at 09:00 local time, the UN could suspend negotiations until next year. That's only happened once since COP meetings began in 1995, so the political pressure to avoid that outcome is immense. Teams have already been rescheduling flights, a telltale sign that a breakthrough remains uncertain.

France's Minister of Ecological Transition Monique Barbut has also raised concerns about carbon capture and storage technology provisions in the text, arguing they provide cover for countries to keep burning fossil fuels. The latest draft has reduced these commitments, which may help, but environmental groups remain unconvinced anything meaningful will emerge.

"I can tell you without any doubt that this has been the worst COP ever," said Mohamed Adow of Powershift Africa. When even seasoned climate observers are losing faith, you know the summit has failed to deliver on its basic promise.

The UK's chief negotiator, Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh, struck a more hopeful tone, suggesting her team was still working toward agreement. But hope and actual climate action are two different things, and [news](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) outlets have been documenting how these summits consistently produce watered-down compromises that fail to match the scale of the crisis.

The question facing delegates isn't really whether they'll reach a deal. It's whether whatever emerges will be worth the paper it's printed on, or just another in a long line of agreements that allowed wealthy nations to maintain the status quo while developing countries face an increasingly uninhabitable planet.
