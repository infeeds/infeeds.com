A nationwide flight suspension that grounded thousands of aircraft and stranded countless passengers across the US was triggered by a faulty copper wire upgrade, Transport Secretary Sean Duffy confirmed at a press conference. The Friday incident represents the most significant air traffic disruption since September 11, 2001, and its effects continue to ripple through the aviation system days later.

The culprit? An outdated telecommunications infrastructure that the US government has long struggled to modernize. Duffy explained that maintenance work was underway to transition from aging copper wiring to modern fiber optics, a project being carried out by technology contractor L3Harris. During this upgrade, a router malfunctioned and began flooding other air traffic control systems, creating communication breakdowns between planes and ground control.

## What Went Wrong?

Facing a potential safety crisis with compromised communications, federal authorities made the only choice they could: halt all flights from the continental US. The ground stop lasted just over four hours starting at 15:54 EST on Friday, but the operational chaos continued for days. More than 3,500 flights were cancelled and over 2,000 delayed on Saturday alone, according to Flight Aware data.

What makes this particularly frustrating is that it didn't have to happen. Duffy is now calling for Congress to approve much-needed infrastructure upgrades, warning that similar incidents are inevitable without action. President Donald Trump has requested $30.1bn for these modernizations, but approval remains uncertain despite Republicans controlling both chambers of Congress.

The FAA and government officials quickly ruled out cyber-attacks and AI-related incidents as causes. Bryan Bedford, head of the Federal Aviation Administration, stated bluntly: "We see zero evidence of anything nefarious, and zero evidence of anything AI related." However, the investigation continues to determine whether the failure stems from contractor incompetence or faulty equipment itself.

## An Ancient System in Crisis

L3Harris revealed it had already completed over 70% of the modernization work before the shutdown occurred. The company acknowledged the severity of the situation in a statement, saying it takes responsibility seriously and is cooperating fully with the FAA investigation. But here's the real problem: the US continues relying on telecommunications infrastructure so outdated that copper wiring was literally being discontinued when this failure happened.

This isn't just an inconvenience for travelers, though the impact on passengers was severe. Some were trapped on airport tarmacs for hours, unable to take off or return to gates. International flights were even given priority departures in an attempt to clear space and manage the cascading cancellations.

Former TSA Administrator John Pistole crystallized the anxiety many people now feel, telling the BBC: "The fact that they were able to get it restarted in four-and-a-half hours is good news, but what's to say it's not going to happen tomorrow?" That's the real fear driving Duffy's calls for congressional action on infrastructure spending.

The broader implication here extends beyond [aviation](https://infeeds.com/tags/aviation). An aging telecommunications backbone puts countless critical systems at risk. When a country's airspace can be suspended because of copper wire problems, it's a sign that critical infrastructure needs urgent attention. Yet getting Congress to prioritize spending on unglamorous but essential upgrades remains perpetually difficult.

The contrast is stark: the US is so technologically advanced in so many ways, yet dependent on communications systems that belong in a museum. The ground stop serves as a wake-up call about how vulnerable modern systems can be when built on crumbling foundations. Until Congress acts on infrastructure modernization, travelers should probably prepare for more disruptions. [News](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) of additional delays and cancellations seems inevitable.
