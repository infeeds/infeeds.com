OpenAI just stepped into the hardware game with Micro, a specialty keypad designed to work alongside ChatGPT. The device, built in collaboration with Work Louder, represents the company's first foray into physical products. But the reception has been decidedly mixed, with some calling it brilliant and others labeling it overpriced vaporware.

## What You're Actually Getting

Micro is a compact keypad with six customizable "agent" keys at the top and six command keys below them. The device connects via Bluetooth or USB cable and lets you assign specific ChatGPT tasks to individual keys. You can toggle between different projects, control agents, and even use voice dictation by holding down a dedicated button.

The build quality is solid. We're talking genuinely sturdy hardware here, with frosted keys and a minimalist design that, if we're being honest, screams Apple aesthetics. The packaging certainly doesn't help dispel that impression.

## The Learning Curve Nobody Asked For

Here's where things get tricky. Micro demands you memorize its color-coded system: white for idle agents, blue for thinking, green for completed tasks, and red for errors. You'll also need to remember which specific projects are assigned to which keys. It's not intuitive, and that's a problem.

The customization happens inside ChatGPT itself through a dedicated Micro tab. Brightness, commands, and project assignments can all be tweaked, but only if you're willing to spend real time learning the system. Early reviewers on Reddit weren't impressed. One user called it "a prank and not a real product." Others argued serious coders would skip it entirely. An independent tech outlet was even harsher, charging that the $230 price tag couldn't be justified when cheaper DIY alternatives exist.

## Does It Actually Work?

Once you crack the learning curve, something interesting happens. The keypad becomes genuinely useful. Toggling between ChatGPT sessions is faster than managing multiple browser tabs. Combined with the voice dictation button, Micro can streamline workflows for people who live in ChatGPT. The efficiency gains are real, assuming you're using the platform constantly.

But here's the uncomfortable question Micro raises: Why spend a week learning a new device when your laptop keyboard already does everything? The value proposition only clicks if you're already a ChatGPT power user who spends hours daily with the AI. For casual users, it's dead weight.

## Context Matters

OpenAI's hardware ambitions arrive wrapped in controversy. Apple recently sued the company over alleged trade theft, and reports suggest OpenAI is developing additional devices that involve former Apple engineers. Whether Micro survives this legal storm remains uncertain, but it's safe to say the timing couldn't be more fraught.

The keypad itself isn't necessarily a bad product. It's just poorly positioned. It targets [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) enthusiasts and hardcore coders, yet neither group seems particularly excited. The former might appreciate the novelty, but the latter demands proven utility. Micro exists in an awkward middle ground where it's neither essential nor particularly affordable.

## The Real Test

Your mileage with Micro depends entirely on your relationship with AI. If ChatGPT is central to your daily work, if you're experimenting with agents and coding tasks, if you appreciate thoughtfully designed hardware with satisfying mechanical feedback, then $230 might feel reasonable. You might even enjoy the experience.

If you're not a power user, don't bother. The learning curve, the price tag, and the friction of adopting new workflows don't justify the investment. Stick with your keyboard and mouse.

The real question OpenAI should be asking itself isn't whether Micro works, but whether anyone actually needed it in the first place.
