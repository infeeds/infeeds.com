The Trump administration has officially reversed one of the last remaining restrictions on TikTok, allowing federal employees to use the app on government-issued devices once again. An Office of Management and Budget memo confirms that "TikTok may be used on government devices," marking a dramatic reversal from the 2022 ban that prohibited access across most federal agencies.

The shift hinges on a Justice Department opinion stating that the 2022 law no longer applies because TikTok now operates under American ownership and uses a different recommendation algorithm than the version that raised national security alarms under ByteDance's control. It's a technical argument that essentially says the app everyone uses today is not the same app that concerned lawmakers and intelligence officials just years ago.

## From Ban to Official Accounts

The change is already rippling through federal agencies. The Department of Transportation, Treasury Department, and Health and Human Services all launched official TikTok accounts last month following the Justice Department's opinion. The White House itself opened an official account last year, seemingly anticipating this exact moment.

It's a striking turnaround considering the intensity of the national security debate that preceded the ban. Under the Biden administration, FBI Director Chris Wray and other intelligence officials argued that TikTok posed a genuine threat to Americans, though they notably never publicly detailed the specific risks. Lawmakers focused on the app's data collection practices and raised concerns about whether China could weaponize its recommendation algorithm for propaganda purposes.

The 2022 legislation reflected genuine bipartisan anxiety about foreign influence and data privacy. Yet the actual nature of the threat remained frustratingly opaque, even to Congress. Was it about targeted surveillance? Influence operations? Behavioral manipulation? The government's refusal to be specific made it nearly impossible to evaluate whether the concern was existential or exaggerated.

## The New TikTok Structure

The current situation is more complicated than a simple "TikTok is now American." The US version operates through TikTok USDS Joint Venture, controlled by investors including Oracle and Silver Lake. However, ByteDance maintains a 19.9 percent stake, meaning Chinese ownership never fully disappeared, just diminished.

This nuance matters. Critics might argue the Justice Department's opinion is as much about political convenience as genuine security assessment. After all, the geopolitical calculation shifted dramatically with a new administration. What was a national security crisis in February 2025 was somehow solved by March 2025, with ownership changes that were already known to be incomplete.

Yet there's another interpretation: Perhaps the original ban was overcautious or based on hypothetical risks that proved less serious than feared. Perhaps having some algorithmic independence and American board representation genuinely does reduce vulnerability to foreign influence. Or perhaps the [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) landscape simply evolved, making the app less of a unique threat among many other data-collecting platforms.

## A Broader Questions About Digital Policy

The speed of this reversal raises uncomfortable questions about how the federal government makes [technology](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) policy. Are these decisions driven by genuine threat assessment, political ideology, or diplomatic relations? If national security officials were truly worried about algorithm manipulation and data harvesting, why would a partial change in ownership eliminate those concerns entirely?

Federal employees now have institutional permission to endlessly scroll TikTok during work hours, accessing content moderated and promoted through systems that may still have Chinese influence. Whether that represents a reasonable compromise or a reversal of hard-won security policy depends largely on your trust in both the technology and the government's assessment of it.

The real test will come in how agencies actually use these accounts and whether they become genuine communication channels or hollow PR exercises.
