An email arrived this summer with a subject line that felt like a missed opportunity. "Magic Key Holder, your Magic Key pass expires in one month." It invited renewal before September 17.

The sender had an eight-year customer who loved Disneyland. Someone who upgraded to the top-tier Inspire Key at $1,749 annually. Someone who stayed at the Grand Californian, booked guided tours, and regularly spent money in the parks. The company had all the data they needed to know this was a valuable customer.

But there was one problem: the timing was tied to a contract date, not to any signal that this customer might be leaving. If Disney had asked for feedback six months earlier, they might have learned why a loyal eight-year holder was quietly planning to walk away. They could have changed course. Instead, they sent a renewal notice to someone who had already made their decision.

## The Problem With Scheduled Feedback

This isn't just a Disney problem. It's everywhere in [business](https://infeeds.com/tags/business), especially in tech and corporate environments. Leaders build feedback processes, put them on a schedule, and receive information that's strategically useless.

One executive found out days before a product launch that his team wouldn't hit their deadline. He came to a coaching session frustrated. But when asked what process he'd actually created for early warnings, the answer was telling: he checked in late in the cycle, after plans were locked, because that was his habit. He had built a system designed to collect confirmation, not truth.

The fix was simple but required discipline. He started checking in earlier, before commitments hardened. He stopped assuming timelines and started asking directly. Most launches have hit their dates since. For the ones that didn't, he had weeks to course-correct instead of days to panic.

The real win? He avoided telling his executive team and customers about delays. That's what had actually frustrated him.

## Timing Changes Everything

Consider the exit survey. A departing employee fills out a form in their final week, and the feedback lands after they're gone. The exercise is reasonable. The timing makes it pointless. You're collecting a summary of decisions people have already made, with zero chance to influence anything.

Annual reviews work the same way. December feedback about June performance gets filed away. The person receiving it has already decided whether they're staying, whether they're engaged, whether they're looking elsewhere.

Here's the hard truth: if what you learn won't change the outcome, you're not gathering feedback. You're keeping records.

## Set Expectations Before Crisis

Another client climbed from senior engineer to vice president. His title changed repeatedly, but his former peers kept taking questions to his boss instead of to him. They didn't see him as their manager yet, even though organizationally, he was.

Instead of waiting for this to become a crisis, he scheduled direct conversations with each person. He opened with honesty: "Our working relationship has shifted. I need to give you feedback as your manager now. How would you like to receive it?" Then he flipped it: "How do you want to give feedback to me?"

He did this before anything went wrong. That mattered. Sorting out how feedback flows between people is an easy conversation when nothing's at stake. Within months, his team brought issues to him first.

## The Real Choice

Leaders often feel frustrated that feedback isn't reaching them. The solution isn't better forms or more formal processes. It's asking earlier and asking more often. Ask at a time when people can still act on what you hear. Ask when you can still change something.

Disney might have kept that eight-year customer if they'd checked in not one month before expiration, but six months before. A team hits deadlines when their leader asks about progress before the plan is locked. Employees stay when feedback flows before they've already mentally quit.

You have a choice: be frustrated that the feedback you want isn't reaching you, or go get it.
