## When Love Becomes a Legal Battle

David and Sara LeBlanc never imagined that displaying giant metal roosters in their yard would land them in federal court. Yet here they are, suing the town of Ogunquit, Maine, over what they say is governmental discrimination against their family and a symbol of their love for their daughter, Pyper.

The roughly 6-foot-tall tin roosters sit at the center of an unusual First Amendment fight. The LeBlancs say the statues comfort their autistic daughter. The town says they're illegal advertising for the couple's adjacent restaurant, the Omelette Factory.

"The roosters are a symbol of one thing: How much we love our daughter," David LeBlanc said in a statement. "But the town made up an entirely different meaning for them."

## The Fight Over Content-Based Regulation

What started as a code enforcement issue has evolved into something bigger. After issuing multiple fines, Ogunquit granted the couple a disability variance allowing two roosters if they were attached to a railing. When the LeBlancs declined and chained them to posts instead, more fines followed. They eventually removed all the statues.

The lawsuit, filed by the Institute for Justice, argues the town is engaging in unconstitutional content-based discrimination. The LeBlancs point out that other local businesses display lobster buoys, antique farm equipment, and large tiki statues without facing fines. Why single out their roosters?

"This is governmental discrimination based on the content of speech and the identity of the speaker," the lawsuit states. "Such discrimination is presumptively unconstitutional, and the Town cannot justify it."

The distinction matters legally and philosophically. When governments regulate speech based on its message rather than its medium, courts scrutinize those rules intensely. The town essentially decided that roosters near a restaurant mean advertising, while the same visual display elsewhere means something else entirely.

## A Victory Down the Road

The LeBlancs aren't alone in this fight. The Institute for Justice, their legal representative, won a similar case in New Hampshire just last year. A Conway judge sided with a bakery owner whose building featured a large mural painted by high school students. The town had argued the mural depicting doughnuts and baked goods was a regulated sign. The court disagreed, finding the town had infringed on free speech rights.

These victories suggest a broader judicial shift toward protecting expressive conduct and [health](https://infeeds.com/tags/health)-related accommodations against overzealous municipal regulation. When disability needs intersect with artistic expression, the balance tilts increasingly toward the individual.

## A Daughter's Simple Joy

What makes this case compelling is its human dimension. Every time Pyper spots a rooster in someone's yard, her face lights up. The statues became meaningful to a child with autism, and parents naturally wanted to preserve that joy.

Something shifted in the community after the roosters were removed. Many of the LeBlancs' original statues were adopted by local residents and businesses. Supporters around the world began ordering their own metal roosters to display. What started as a family accommodation became a grass roots movement.

"This town has embraced her," Sara LeBlanc said. "We wish the town government would, too."

That gap between community support and [disability](https://infeeds.com/tags/disability) acceptance highlights how easily bureaucratic rules can override human empathy. A town clerk concerned about sign codes may not see a child's comfort. She sees a violation. The legal system must sometimes force that recalibration.

Town clerk Christine Murphy did not respond to requests for comment. The legal battle ahead will determine whether municipalities can prohibit expressive displays based on their perceived commercial context, or whether such regulation crosses constitutional lines when disability accommodations are involved.
