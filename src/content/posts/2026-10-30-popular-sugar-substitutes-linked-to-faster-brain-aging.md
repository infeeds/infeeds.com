The promise of guilt-free sweetness has made artificial sweeteners a staple in modern diets. Yet a sobering new study from the American Academy of Neurology suggests that this trade-off may come at a significant cognitive cost.

Researchers analyzing data from nearly 13,000 Brazilian adults over eight years found that high consumption of low- and no-calorie sweeteners was associated with dramatically faster declines in memory and thinking skills. People in the highest consumption group experienced a 62% faster cognitive decline compared to those who consumed the least. That's equivalent to roughly 1.6 additional years of brain aging over the study period.

## Which Sweeteners Are Raising Concerns?

The study examined seven commonly used sweeteners: aspartame, saccharin, acesulfame-K, erythritol, xylitol, sorbitol and tagatose. These ingredients populate the ingredient lists of diet sodas, flavored waters, yogurts and low-calorie desserts. Six of the seven showed associations with faster cognitive decline, with tagatose being the lone exception. Sorbitol, a sugar alcohol frequently used in diet products, had the highest average intake among study participants.

What makes this finding particularly striking is that even moderate consumption showed measurable effects. Those in the middle consumption group experienced a 35% faster decline in cognitive abilities compared to low consumers. "Low- and no-calorie sweeteners are often seen as a healthy alternative to sugar; however, our findings suggest certain sweeteners may have negative effects on brain health over time," said study author Claudia Kimie Suemoto, MD, PhD.

The cognitive tests measured multiple brain functions essential for daily life: memory recall, verbal fluency, processing speed and working memory. Participants completed these assessments at the beginning, middle and end of the eight-year study period, allowing researchers to track changes over time.

## Who Is Most at Risk?

Age matters significantly. Younger adults, particularly those under 60, showed stronger associations between sweetener consumption and cognitive decline. Notably, this connection was weaker or absent among older participants. The findings were also more pronounced in people with diabetes, a group particularly likely to reach for artificial sweeteners to manage their sugar intake.

This is especially relevant because diabetics are frequently advised to limit sugar consumption. If they're substituting sugar with sweeteners that may damage [brain function](https://infeeds.com/tags/neurology), the strategy could backfire.

Researchers were careful to note a crucial limitation: this study shows association, not causation. We cannot conclude from this research alone that sweeteners directly damage the brain. Other factors could explain the relationship, and people's dietary memories aren't always accurate. Participants estimated their consumption based on recollection of foods eaten over the previous year, introducing potential inaccuracy.

The observational design means confounding variables may play a role. Perhaps people who consume more artificial sweeteners have other lifestyle factors contributing to cognitive decline. Perhaps they're more likely to have undiagnosed metabolic issues or other health conditions affecting brain function.

## What Comes Next?

More research is urgently needed. The current study didn't cover every artificial sweetener on the market, so findings can't be universally applied. Dr. Suemoto noted that alternative sweetening options like applesauce, honey, maple syrup or coconut sugar warrant investigation as potential healthier substitutes.

These results challenge the assumption that artificial sweeteners are automatically better than sugar simply because they contain fewer calories. The real question becomes: what are we trading when we choose the diet version?

For those concerned about cognitive health and who currently rely on sweetened foods and beverages, the prudent approach might be reducing sweetness overall, whether from sugar or substitutes. The brain may benefit more from retraining taste preferences toward less sweet foods entirely than from swapping one sweet chemical for another. Understanding the long-term consequences of what we consume requires patience, but this research suggests that patience is something our brains may need to maintain.
