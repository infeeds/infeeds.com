The International Court of Justice made history Thursday when it ruled that Myanmar's military committed genocide against the Rohingya Muslim minority. In a 9-6 decision, judges determined that the 2017 military crackdown was carried out with explicit intent to destroy this long-persecuted group, marking the first time in the court's 80-year history that it has held a state responsible for genocide.

The ruling, brought by Gambia under the 1948 Genocide Convention, carries enormous symbolic weight for the roughly 900,000 Rohingya who have suffered decades of discrimination and violence. For many refugees now living in Bangladesh's sprawling camps, the judgment felt like vindication.

"I feel wonderful and grateful," said Ma Roga Bibi, 18, who fled Maungdaw township in 2017 and now lives in the world's largest refugee settlement. "It was an emotional, historic day."

But even as she celebrated, Bibi's next words revealed a harder truth: recognition alone isn't enough.

## The Scale of Violence

During several weeks in August and September 2017, Myanmar's military systematically attacked and burned hundreds of Rohingya villages to the ground. Soldiers committed widespread rapes, massacred civilians including children, and destroyed 392 villages either completely or partially.

The ICJ estimated approximately 10,000 Rohingya were killed, with up to 2,109 children under 5 among the dead. Judge Yuji Iwasawa, the court's president, specifically noted that "the execution of Rohingya men and boys in some locations, the widespread sexual violence, the targeting of children and babies, the methodical burning of villages, and attacks on fleeing civilians are not consistent with an intent to conduct counterinsurgency operations."

Myanmar had argued its military was conducting legitimate counterinsurgency against an armed group called the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army. The court rejected this defense entirely, finding that the military failed to distinguish between civilians and alleged insurgents and that the scale and nature of violence couldn't possibly be explained by counterterrorism tactics.

## Justice Remains Elusive

While the ruling is legally binding on Myanmar and all states that have ratified the Genocide Convention, enforcement poses a massive challenge. The [news](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) from The Hague doesn't automatically translate to action on the ground.

Khin Khin Myint, 18, a refugee leader with activist group Rohingya Youth Voice, captured the frustration: "We want the world to listen to survivors, recognize their courage, and include women in decisions about our future. We expect the international community to move beyond a statement of sympathy."

The court ordered Myanmar to investigate and prosecute suspected perpetrators, provide restitution and compensation, and enable displaced Rohingya to return home. But these mandates crash against a bleak reality. Rakhine state, where most Rohingya originated, is now controlled by the Arakan Army, an ethnic Rakhine armed group engaged in ongoing conflict with Myanmar's military.

Meanwhile, Rohingya in the camps face threats from multiple armed groups, statelessness, and starvation. Many are "on the verge of acute famine" due to aid shortfalls and the junta's blockade on Rakhine state. Unless the broader conflict dynamics improve or credible safety guarantees materialize from all armed actors, prospects for return look grim.

## International Ripple Effects

The judgment will reverberate far beyond Myanmar. Legal experts note it sets important precedent for future genocide cases, including South Africa's case against Israel, particularly regarding questions of genocidal intent and state responsibility.

The Trump administration has already signaled skepticism, with the State Department questioning whether the ICJ properly interpreted the Genocide Convention and whether the decision might later be "weaponized" against allied forces. This tension highlights how [news](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) from international justice bodies has become increasingly politicized.

Antonia Mulvey, executive director of Legal Action Worldwide, urged countries to review trade agreements and financial ties with Myanmar's junta while supporting accountability and reparations. The question now is whether nations will actually follow through.

For the Rohingya, a court victory feels hollow when your future remains uncertain and your past remains unresolved.
