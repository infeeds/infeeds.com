The Nasdaq Composite has officially entered correction territory, slipping more than 11% below its record high from last month. Thursday's 5% plunge marked a significant turning point for the [tech-heavy index](https://infeeds.com/tags/business), raising questions about what comes next for investors holding growth and semiconductor stocks.

On Wall Street, corrections are measured in precise terms: any decline greater than 10% qualifies as a correction, while a 20% pullback signals a bear market. The Nasdaq is now sitting firmly in that correction zone, making this moment particularly significant for portfolio managers watching their positions.

## The Historical Pattern Points to Bumpy Roads Ahead

But here's what history tells us: more selling could be coming in the very near term. Across 10 previous correction episodes over the past decade, selloffs in 2025, 2022, 2020, and 2018 all experienced additional rough trading over the following month. The pattern isn't uniform, but it's persistent enough to warrant attention.

The silver lining? After that initial turbulence subsides, recoveries have historically taken hold around the three-month mark. The Nasdaq has gained an average of 9.41% from the correction bottom over the following three months and 17.03% over six months. Those figures suggest that patience and discipline could reward investors willing to weather the storm.

There's one notable exception to this recovery narrative: January 2022. That correction offered a considerably harsher outcome, with the Nasdaq losing another 18.3% over the next six months and remaining down 24.3% a year later. It's a sobering reminder that historical averages don't guarantee outcomes.

## Elections and AI Concerns Drive the Selloff

The immediate catalyst for this correction stems from Tuesday's midterm elections, where Democrats swept the House and Senate. The party also secured significant gains in Texas, where gubernatorial candidate Gina Hinojosa won after campaigning on an immediate moratorium for data center development.

Investors are now grappling with whether tighter state-level restrictions could slow data center construction and, by extension, put pressure on companies tied to the artificial intelligence trade. It's a legitimate concern that's rattled confidence in previously high-flying tech stocks.

Chip stocks bore the brunt of Thursday's selling pressure. The iShares Semiconductor ETF (SOXX) fell 12%, marking its worst day since the Covid-19 breakout of March 2020. When the semiconductor sector experiences that kind of decline in a single session, it signals real fear flowing through the market.

## Midterm Cycles Offer Guidance, Not Guarantees

Frank Cappelleri, founder of CappThesis, studied past midterm election cycles for perspective. His research revealed that the Nasdaq was higher 12 months after the election in all but one of 13 midterm cycles since 1974. That's a compelling statistic, suggesting long-term resilience even when near-term uncertainty reigns.

Yet Cappelleri was quick to add an important caveat: "The path can be a volatile one." He pointed to 2018 as a case study. The Nasdaq actually rallied the day after Democrats won the House, but a sharp December sell-off followed. Despite that volatility, the index was ultimately about 15% higher a year after the election.

The message seems clear: corrections are uncomfortable, but they're not necessarily catastrophic for patient investors with longer time horizons. The key is distinguishing between normal market turbulence and structural deterioration in the [economy](https://infeeds.com/tags/business) or corporate earnings.

For now, investors should prepare for the possibility of further weakness in the coming weeks. History suggests that capitulation often comes before recovery, and the semiconductor sector's sharp decline on Thursday hints that sellers still have conviction. But those who can tolerate near-term pain should remember that corrections, while painful, have consistently rewarded long-term resolve.

The question isn't whether the Nasdaq will recover, but rather whether you can handle the inevitable ups and downs along the way.
