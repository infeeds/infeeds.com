The U.S. is experiencing a measles crisis that hasn't been seen in three decades. With 3,471 cases currently documented across the country and four deaths this year, health officials are sounding the alarm. What's particularly alarming is that this outbreak isn't just affecting children. About 38% to 40% of measles cases this year have struck adults, a fact many people overlook when thinking about this resurgent disease.

The numbers tell a stark story. We've gone from 85 cases in 2000 to 2,289 cases in 2024, and now to over 3,471 in 2025. One of the deaths this year was a 40-year-old unvaccinated woman, proving that measles can be deadly at any age.

## Why Measles Is Back and So Dangerous

Measles had been virtually eliminated from the U.S. in the 2000s. For years, any cases that appeared were imported by travelers returning from overseas. But now, domestic outbreaks are happening with frightening regularity. The reason is simple: measles is one of the most contagious illnesses known to mankind. You need only a small amount of virus to become infected.

The complications can be devastating. Beyond the characteristic rash, measles can cause pneumonia, brain swelling (encephalitis), and death. Adults aren't immune to these severe outcomes. In fact, just over half of measles hospitalizations in 2025 were adults, not children.

"We often forget about adults getting measles," notes Dr. Gavin Harris from Emory Healthcare. "Adults become infected at the same, if not higher, rates than children themselves."

## Do You Need a Booster Shot?

The good news is that the MMR vaccine (measles, mumps, and rubella) is highly effective. Two doses provide about 97% protection, while a single dose offers 93% protection. Here's the critical part: if you received two doses of the MMR vaccine after your first birthday, you almost certainly don't need a booster.

Most people who attended public schools have been vaccinated. If you have documented proof of two doses, you're protected for life. But what if you can't find your records? That's where things get tricky.

## Finding Your Vaccination Records

If you have no idea whether you were vaccinated, start by checking your state's immunization registry online. Many pharmacists and healthcare providers can also look up your records in their statewide systems. If those avenues fail, try contacting your parents or your childhood doctor's office.

If absolutely no records exist, the recommendation is straightforward: get an MMR dose. It's completely safe to receive another shot even if you were vaccinated decades ago but have no documentation.

Another option is a titer test, a blood test that looks for antibodies from past vaccines or diseases. However, experts note this approach adds complexity and cost through lab fees, copays, and travel time. For most adults, simply getting another dose makes more practical sense.

## Special Considerations for Certain Age Groups

Adults born before 1957 are considered immune because nearly everyone contracted measles in childhood before the vaccine existed. However, adults who received the vaccine between 1963 and 1967 should reconsider their protection status. That version of the vaccine wasn't fully effective, so those individuals may need a booster.

The broader message from [health](https://infeeds.com/tags/health) experts is clear: vaccination is the absolute best way to prevent measles. If you're unvaccinated and exposed, you have a narrow window to respond. The measles vaccine provides protection within three days of exposure, and IVIG immunoglobulin offers protection within six days.

With measles spreading domestically at rates not seen in 30 years, assuming you're protected isn't enough. Either track down your vaccination records or talk to your doctor about getting updated. The stakes are too high for guesswork, especially when outbreaks are happening around you with increasing frequency.
