The US Department of Commerce is quietly circulating a proposed rule that would fundamentally reshape how America counts itself. According to documents reviewed by WIRED, the plan would eliminate census questions about race, ethnicity, and sexual orientation while excluding undocumented immigrants from the 2030 count entirely.

The stated rationale is remarkably vague: the rule says these changes would protect the census from "distortions created from the inclusion of personal questions." But experts and federal employees who spoke anonymously to WIRED say the real implications are far more consequential.

## The Real Stakes: Federal Funding and Political Power

This isn't academic. Census data determines everything from how congressional seats get apportioned to how federal funding flows to states and localities. Remove certain populations from the count, and you remove their political representation and their access to resources.

"If we don't know that certain peoples live in a particular geography, we can't say, 'Oh my gosh, here's some massive inequality,'" says Danah Boyd, a Cornell University professor who studies the census. "If we don't know certain things, we can't challenge them politically."

An HHS employee told WIRED that many federal health programs rely on census data to allocate funding. Removing undocumented immigrants from the count would hit localities with high immigrant populations particularly hard. Native populations could lose specialized funding tied to the "American Indian or Alaska Native" census category.

The proposal also likely requires reintroducing a citizenship question for the first time since 1950. The Trump administration attempted this in 2020, but the Supreme Court struck it down 5-4, with Chief Justice John Roberts calling the government's justification "contrived."

## The Chilling Effect

Here's where things get darker. A Commerce Department employee expressed a concern that extends beyond policy mechanics: fear. If a citizenship question appears on the census, undocumented immigrants and their mixed-status families may simply refuse to participate. Even without explicit threats, the perception that answering could trigger ICE enforcement would suppress participation rates.

"When you eliminate people and when you make people too afraid to participate because of the eliminations of other peoples, you will actually purposefully suppress the number of people who are in a geography," Boyd explains. "And that is a way of trying to make certain that you erase their political power and their access to federal funding."

This connects directly to a broader campaign against census accuracy. Republicans have attacked "differential privacy," the statistical method used to prevent reidentification of individual respondents in published data. They've also pushed the COUNT Act, which would require the citizenship question. Senator Jim Banks even argued that the Constitution's framers intended the census to cover only "lawful inhabitants."

## Context Matters

The timing is significant. Earlier this year, the Supreme Court gutted the Voting Rights Act, one of the most powerful tools for challenging discriminatory voting practices. Removing the demographic data needed to identify voting rights violations means no data exists to make those legal challenges. Connect those dots and the picture becomes clearer: without racial demographic information from the census, proving that congressional districts have been drawn to dilute minority voting power becomes nearly impossible.

The Department of Commerce has declined to comment on the proposed rule, which is currently circulating through government agencies for review. But census questions are typically set two to three years before the actual survey, and they're based on priorities set by the administration.

We've seen this movie before. During earlier administrations, [census](https://infeeds.com/tags/census) undergoes its own politicization cycles. But what makes this proposal different is its explicit goal: not just to ask different questions, but to render entire populations statistically invisible to federal decision-making.

The question isn't whether this rule will face legal challenges. It almost certainly will. The question is whether demographic data collection will survive them, or whether the next census becomes a tool for erasing rather than understanding America's actual population.
