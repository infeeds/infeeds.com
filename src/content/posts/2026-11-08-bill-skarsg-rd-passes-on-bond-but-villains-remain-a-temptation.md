Bill Skarsgård won't be slipping into a tuxedo to save the world as the next James Bond, but honestly, he seems pretty okay with that. The Swedish actor recently appeared on Josh Horowitz's "Happy Sad Confused" podcast to address the mounting speculation about who will take over the iconic 007 role in Denis Villeneuve's upcoming reboot. When asked about it, Skarsgård had a refreshingly candid take: they simply didn't call.

"They didn't call me about James Bond. I don't know what that was about," he joked on the podcast. "Every single British, Australian [actor] ... but maybe they were like, 'We can't do a Swede for this guy.' I wasn't even thrown into the mix."

It's a fair point. Bond casting has historically favored British talent, and the recent wave of [entertainment](https://infeeds.com/tags/entertainment) rumors has largely stuck to that tradition. Names like Callum Turner, Josh O'Connor, and Jack Lowden have dominated the conversation, with the internet recently buzzing over reports that Robert Pattinson and Nicholas Hoult actually screen-tested for the role.

## Why Bond Doesn't Appeal

But here's the thing: Skarsgård doesn't seem bothered by the omission. When Horowitz suggested that committing to Bond for a decade-plus might not be ideal anyway, Skarsgård enthusiastically agreed. "That's right," he said. "Just want to go in and do one Mads Mikkelsen villain."

That's a telling statement. Mikkelsen's portrayal of Le Chiffre in "Casino Royale" remains one of the most memorable Bond villain performances, precisely because it was a singular, focused turn. Skarsgård clearly values that kind of creative autonomy over the sprawling commitment that comes with playing the world's most famous spy.

The actor is already plenty familiar with playing complex, menacing characters. His filmography reads like a greatest hits of modern villainy: Pennywise in the "It" movies, Count Orlok in Robert Eggers' atmospheric "Nosferatu," Eric Draven in the recent "The Crow" reboot, and the deliciously theatrical Marquis Vincent Bisset de Gramont in "John Wick: Chapter 4."

## The Art of Choosing Roles

Yet despite that extensive resume of dark characters, Skarsgård is becoming more selective about which villainous roles he takes on. When discussing his creative process, he explained that he doesn't really approach role selection methodically. Instead, he feels "drawn or compelled" to projects in ways that defy easy explanation.

"It's the weird thing," he told Horowitz. "A lot of people ask me, what goes into picking your roles or whatever and I don't really know how to say it other than I feel drawn or compelled to. It's something that you can't really put your words on it and describe why it's happening."

That intuitive approach has clearly served him well. But Skarsgård is also mindful of the creative fatigue that can come with playing too many similar characters. "I've also noticed that I don't want to become jaded," he said. "Which means that I think I could probably go long stretches without working and waiting for something to be compelling enough for me to work on."

It's a refreshingly mature perspective in an industry that often rewards actors for maximizing their output. Skarsgård is essentially saying he'd rather wait for the right project than churn out mediocre work just to stay visible.

## The Future of Villainy

When it comes to villain roles specifically, he's become even more discerning. "For a villain, it would have to be something that felt original to me, that was something that I hadn't done before," he explained. "I feel, kind of, spent with those things. But there are villains [that] could be really fun in different ways and if it's the right kind."

This suggests Skarsgård isn't retiring from [entertainment](https://infeeds.com/tags/entertainment) or villain roles entirely. Rather, he's signaling that the next antagonist he portrays needs to offer something genuinely fresh. No paint-by-numbers dark characters, no retreading familiar ground.

In a landscape where actors often chase franchise opportunities and paydays, Skarsgård's stance is almost contrarian. He'd rather stay home and wait for inspiration than sleepwalk through a Bond film. That kind of principled approach to one's craft might not fill the box office, but it almost certainly produces better art.
