## The Anti-AI That's Actually a Person

What if you stopped asking ChatGPT your questions and texted a random stranger instead? That's the premise behind ChatTJB, a San Francisco-based project that launched in April but didn't explode until a $6,000 billboard went up in July. The billboard's tagline? "The leading chat interface powered by AI"—where AI stands for "average individual."

Behind ChatTJB is 32-year-old Tucker Bryant, a former Google project manager turned artist and public speaker. Unlike traditional AI chatbots trained on mountains of data, Bryant literally sits down and answers every single prompt himself. Need a drawing of a skunk wearing sunglasses? He'll sketch one. Wondering how to fry an egg? He'll suggest something. Maybe. (WIRED asked how to become a morning person and never got a response, with the website noting that "thousands of people are writing in right now.") By August 6, the project had received over 30,000 queries, peaking at around 5,000 prompts per hour.

## More Than Just a Joke

Bryant insists this is an art project first, not just a funny bit or social experiment. "It was sort of an opportunity for me to provide a little bit of commentary on a strange moment that I feel like we're in," he tells WIRED. He's not anti-AI—he actually used AI to code the ChatTJB website—but he's deeply concerned about what he calls "cognitive surrender."

Cognitive surrender is the tendency to accept AI outputs with decreasing amounts of critical thinking. Bryant describes his own wake-up moment: asking an LLM whether to wear short or long sleeves on a 65-degree San Francisco day. "I've been living in this city for seven years," he realized. "I know how to handle the weather. And yet, it felt easier to defer to an LLM for advice."

That uncomfortable realization became the spark for ChatTJB. As more people use these tools, the risk of outsourcing basic judgment increases. The project forces people to confront that dynamic in real time.

## What People Actually Ask

The queries have ranged from absurd to genuinely personal. Bryant has drawn a frog eating spaghetti, San Francisco Mayor David Lurie as Godzilla, and black cats eating grass on a table. But the written requests reveal something deeper. People ask about birthday alternatives, dinner ideas, or how to use leftover ingredients. Some even share genuine personal concerns.

"A lot of people initially interacted with this as a joke," Bryant explains. "But when they realized they could have actual conversation, some responses became more personal, sincere, and genuine."

He's quick to note that he's not a particularly skilled artist and his advice isn't expert-level—he literally called it "the worst LLM of all time" on YouTube. But that's precisely the point. "The value is getting a human to bounce questions off of, as opposed to getting super expert advice," he says. "It's very tender to see."

## The Art of Slowing Down

Bryant initially expected the project to last a few days. But after witnessing people's enthusiasm, he's keeping it going with help from 10 carefully selected volunteers (chosen from 3,000 applicants). There's even a [ChatTJB Pro option](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) for $5 monthly, though Bryant is brutally honest: "This is a worthless tier." Users get nothing but knowledge they're supporting art. Four people have paid so far.

The $6,000 billboard, staying up through late August, hasn't been financially recouped. But that was never the goal. "I really just want people to have an opportunity to take a second look at both their own relationship to technology as well as the world's relationship to technology," Bryant says.

In an era of constant claims about world-changing [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) alongside frightening AI bubble coverage, ChatTJB offers something rare: levity and reflection. "I hope that makes a weird time sit a little bit lighter," Bryant adds, "because I think sometimes that's the best that we can do."

Maybe the real value of ChatTJB isn't the answers themselves, but the pause they create—a moment to ask why we trusted the algorithm in the first place.
