A woman's life-threatening allergic reaction to an antibiotic transmitted through her partner's semen has prompted urgent calls from clinicians to update patient information leaflets and medical training programs.

The incident unfolded when the woman's partner, who was taking amoxicillin to treat a sinus infection, asked his doctor whether it was safe for the couple to have unprotected sex given her known allergy to penicillins. He received explicit reassurance that she would not be at risk. Six hours after taking the antibiotic, he and his partner had penetrative vaginal sex without protection.

Within minutes of ejaculation, everything changed. "Everything started to swell. I couldn't breathe," the woman recalled to New Scientist. She had developed anaphylaxis, a severe allergic reaction that can be fatal without immediate treatment. She self-administered an EpiPen, injecting adrenaline to halt the reaction, then received additional doses from paramedics before being hospitalized. She was discharged after six hours.

## How Antibiotics End Up in Semen

Cassim Akhoon, a clinician at King's College Hospital in London who examined the case, explains the biological mechanism. Antibiotics enter the bloodstream and can reach the prostate and seminal vesicles, the pouch-like glands behind the bladder that produce most seminal fluid. Additionally, amoxicillin is excreted in urine, meaning it can persist in the urethra until flushed out during ejaculation.

About a year after the incident, Akhoon's team confirmed the woman's penicillin allergy through skin testing, strengthening their conclusion that semen transmission was the cause. This wasn't an isolated case either. Earlier reports describe a woman who developed anaphylaxis after exposure to amoxicillin during oral sex.

Yet despite these documented cases, healthcare workers remain largely unaware of the risk. The woman says medical staff treating her seemed puzzled, with some assuming she had taken the drug herself. "I wasn't taught about this in medical school," Akhoon admits. "I definitely think we should make this part of formal medical training."

## The Case for Updated Information

Akhoon argues the evidence is strong enough to warrant inclusion on [medication leaflets](https://infeeds.com/tags/health). "If you read any drug leaflet, they list a whole bunch of super-rare risks, so it's not unreasonable to also include this risk on those leaflets to boost awareness," he says. Amoxicillin's leaflet already mentions aseptic meningitis, a rare side effect where tissue around the brain and spinal cord becomes inflamed, despite less mechanistic proof than exists for semen transmission.

The team has submitted findings to the UK's Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency for consideration of leaflet updates. Once informed of the risk, people could take simple preventive measures: wearing condoms or abstaining from sex during their antibiotic course.

Chris Rutkowski, a consultant allergist in London, emphasizes that medical training should prioritize this knowledge. "Not because it's common, but because the cost of missing it is high," he notes. Patrick Yong, who also examined the case, agrees that education and awareness represent the best defense.

## Looking Forward

Currently, about 1 percent of people have penicillin allergies confirmed via skin-prick tests, though anaphylaxis from exposure remains extremely rare. There isn't yet sufficient evidence to flag this risk for other [antibiotics and drug classes](https://infeeds.com/tags/antibiotics), but Akhoon suggests this may change. "As people consider this route of transmission more, we might start to get a bigger collection of cases to determine which drug classes are implicated," he says.

There is also no evidence that antibiotics transferred via vaginal fluid pose similar risks to sexual partners, he adds.

This case highlights a critical gap in medical knowledge and patient safety information. A routine doctor's visit, a simple antibiotic prescription, and a reassurance that should have been safe instead nearly claimed a woman's life. The oversight wasn't malicious or negligent in the traditional sense, but rather reflects how uncommon scenarios can slip through the cracks of medical education and standard protocols.
