Sid Meier's four-year-old grandson doesn't play Civilization, but he can't stop singing "Baba Yetu." The preschooler belts out the franchise's iconic theme song day and night, videos and all. It's a small moment that perfectly encapsulates what Meier has witnessed over the past three decades: an intergenerational love affair with one of gaming's most enduring franchises.

"What I've seen more recently is this intergenerational connection," Meier tells Variety. "I'll talk to a parent and they'll say, I introduced my daughter or son to Civ, and now we play together."

With Civilization approaching its 35th anniversary this September and Meier's studio Firaxis hitting 30 years, the numbers alone tell an impressive story. But what makes this legacy truly remarkable is how the franchise has managed to stay relevant in an industry obsessed with chasing the next big thing.

## Staying Power in a Crowded Market

Firexis president Heather Hazen articulates a unique problem that most studios would kill to have: Civilization 6 is the biggest competitor to Civilization 7. Rather than seeing this as a stumbling block, Hazen frames it as an ecosystem challenge. "We want our fans to be playing Civilization, regardless of which installment it is," she explains.

This philosophy extends to how Firaxis approaches [entertainment](https://infeeds.com/tags/entertainment) strategy. The developer isn't racing to make everyone abandon older versions. Instead, they're thinking about how players can engage with Civilization when they have 30 minutes versus 30 hours. It's a refreshingly humble approach that prioritizes player agency over quarterly earnings.

Meier credits the franchise's longevity to a simple internal rule: each new Civilization game needs to justify its existence. "There are usually 5-6-7-year intervals where we build upon the current game with additional civilizations and leaders and different ways of playing in scenarios," he says. When the creative cupboard is full enough to warrant a fresh start, that's when a new iteration launches.

## From SimCity to Turn-Based Perfection

Few people know that the original Civilization prototype wasn't turn-based at all. Meier envisioned something more like SimCity, where you'd zone areas and watch production unfold passively. Halfway through development, everything changed.

"About halfway through, we made the change to turn-based and actually moving things around and having to really pay attention to what you're doing, and all of a sudden, your brain is totally engaged," Meier recalls. That pivot transformed the game. Suddenly, players were making meaningful decisions with time to consider consequences.

When Civilization launched in 1991 with just 14 playable civilizations, Meier had no idea how seriously people would take it. Letters poured in from Canadians wondering why they weren't included. Americans, Aztecs, and Babylonians made the cut, but entire regions and cultures were conspicuously absent.

"We made the original game in a year with three people, so there's only so much we can do then," Meier admits. Over time, as resources grew, so did representation. Civilization 7 has made significant strides with additions like the Shawnee civilization and diverse leaders, reflecting a more inclusive approach to history itself.

## Building the Next 30 Years

Civilization 7 launched in 2025 to mixed reviews, particularly regarding its "Ages" system. Rather than doubling down, Firaxis listened. The team conducted three rounds of public playtesting and released "The Test of Time" update to address concerns. Hazen believes they struck the right balance, restoring that sense of continuity and player agency that longtime fans cherish.

As for what comes next, Meier follows a design principle he calls the "1/3, 1/3, 1/3" rule: one-third classic mechanics, one-third improvements on recent iterations, and one-third entirely new ideas. When that new ideas bucket fills up, it's time for a fresh game.

Meier's greatest lesson after three decades? Never say never. After finishing Civilization One, he thought he was done with strategy games entirely. He pivoted to a music-generating game called "C.P.U. Bach." Then Brian Reynolds pitched Civilization 2 for Windows, introducing moddability. Meier initially resisted the concept.

"My game is perfect," he thought. "There's no reason to do a mod. He was right, and I was wrong."

That humility, that willingness to be proven wrong by the community and by fellow designers, might be the real secret to Civilization's longevity.
