What started as an act of community solidarity has become something far more desperate. In the besieged Yemeni city of Taiz, women volunteers who began by baking cookies for frontline soldiers have now organized a major fundraising campaign to purchase weapons and ammunition. It's a stark illustration of how prolonged conflict transforms ordinary citizens into active participants in warfare.

The shift happened gradually but inevitably. After nearly four years of relative calm following a UN-brokered truce in April 2022, Houthi forces launched a massive ground offensive in early September, rapidly capturing strategic territory along Yemen's Red Sea coast, including the port city of Mocha. The Iran-backed armed group has effectively surrounded Taiz, strangling the flow of food, fuel, and medicine into the provincial capital.

## From Cookies to Weapons

When the fighting resumed, women organized to support government defenders. Initially, volunteers baked cookies and organized food convoys for troops. Activist Amira al-Saghir explained the original mission: strengthening the bond between the community and soldiers defending their "besieged city, which is subjected to daily shelling in preparation for an invasion."

But as rural districts fell to Houthi control, something shifted. Donors began making their contributions conditional on funds being used for ammunition. Social media filled with claims that government fighters lacked sufficient supplies to hold their positions. The women activists faced a choice: maintain their humanitarian focus or adapt to military necessity.

Huriya Wabel, a humanitarian activist, described the turning point bluntly: "After we saw that those militias are waging a war of extermination against our city, we felt that we had to take a serious stand to support the army, not just with food and clothing, but with weapons as well."

By October 8, the women's department of Taiz's Supreme Council for Popular Resistance launched an ambitious campaign to equip 10,000 pro-government fighters with rifles and ammunition. They called for donations from Yemenis worldwide.

## The Personal Cost of Survival

What makes this effort remarkable isn't just its scope but the sacrifice behind it. In Yemen, the poorest nation in the Middle East and deeply conservative in tradition, women rarely participate in active combat. These initiatives offered them a way to defend their city without holding weapons themselves.

Activist Khadija al-Majidi has made multiple daily trips to frontlines near Jabal Sabir mountain, delivering ammunition worth thousands of dollars along with winter supplies. She's no longer afraid of active battlefronts after a decade living through Yemen's devastating civil war. "Repeated visits to the front lines play a major role in removing fears," she explained.

The personal stories are heartbreaking. One woman donated her wedding ring. Others gave their gold jewelry. Al-Majidi recalled a woman arriving with envelopes of Yemeni and Saudi currency, inscribed with a simple message: "God willing, we will win. Buy ammunition for the heroes with this."

These aren't wealthy donors. They're people already facing humanitarian crisis, giving whatever remains. [News](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) from conflict zones often focuses on military movements and casualty counts, but rarely captures this dimension: ordinary people, already broken by war, choosing to give more.

## The Impossible Choice

When asked why they've shifted from humanitarian work to weapons procurement, activists point to existential threat. Al-Majidi's response was pointed: "When the enemy reaches the gates of your city and imposes a complete siege on it in preparation for storming it by force... then what do you expect from us? Did you expect us to carry flowers?"

It's a question that exposes the brutal logic of siege warfare. Humanitarian aid becomes insufficient when survival itself is threatened. Food and medicine matter little if your city falls to an invading force. [Conflict](https://infeeds.com/tags/conflict) in Yemen has been ongoing since 2014, with no clear resolution despite international diplomatic efforts.

The longer the siege continues, the worse conditions become. Government forces conduct daily counteroffensives against Houthi attacks, but ammunition shortages limit their effectiveness. The women's campaigns represent a population's desperate attempt to bridge that gap through collective sacrifice.

What does it say about modern warfare that defending a city now depends on women selling their wedding rings?
