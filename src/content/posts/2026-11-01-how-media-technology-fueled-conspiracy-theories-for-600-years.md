Every technological revolution promised liberation. Every one delivered something darker too. Al Jazeera's latest Deja Vu episode pulls back the curtain on a pattern stretching back centuries: new media doesn't just spread truth, it supercharges lies.

The printing press was supposed to democratize knowledge. It did. Gutenberg's invention helped ignite the Enlightenment, spreading scientific thinking across Europe. But it also weaponized fear. Those same printing presses churned out pamphlets fueling witch-hunts, transforming local suspicions into mass hysteria. Thousands died because technology made conspiracy thinking contagious.

That contradiction shapes everything that followed. The Nazis understood radio's raw power. They didn't invent propaganda, but they industrialized it. Radio broadcasts reached into homes, into families, making lies feel intimate and authoritative. The medium became the message, and the message was total control.

## Technology Always Cuts Both Ways

Each era thinks it's different. Social media entrepreneurs promised connection and freedom. Platforms would democratize voice, they said. Marginalized communities could finally speak without gatekeepers. And yes, that happened. But dictators were listening too. They learned to use the same tools for surveillance, for control, for spreading disinformation at scale.

The pattern is clear: whoever masters the new technology first gets to shape reality for everyone else.

Now we're staring down artificial intelligence. The podcast asks the question nobody wants to answer: what comes next? We're already seeing deepfakes that can impersonate world leaders. AI can generate perfectly plausible [news](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) articles that never happened. Language models can write conspiracy theories so coherent, so detailed, so personally targeted that distinguishing them from truth becomes nearly impossible.

## The Real Threat Isn't the Technology Itself

It's that we keep making the same mistake. We deploy new tools without understanding their social consequences. We optimize for engagement without considering what gets engaged with most. Anger spreads faster than accuracy. Fear outpaces facts.

The Deja Vu team, including producers Mohamed Hassan, Marcos Bartolome, Marthe van der Wolf, and Sonia Bhagat, traces this through centuries of evidence. It's not optimistic viewing. But it's necessary. Because understanding how we got here might tell us something about where we're headed.

The conspiracy theories themselves don't matter as much as what they reveal about us. They show what we fear. They show what information environments we're embedded in. They show how desperate we become for narrative and meaning when the world feels chaotic.

AI won't create conspiracy theories from nothing. It'll perfect what we've always done: take our suspicions, our grievances, our need to find patterns in chaos, and amplify them until they feel undeniable. It'll personalize them. It'll make them impossible to ignore.

The printing press gave us both science and witch-hunts. Radio gave us both Beethoven and propaganda. Social media gave us both community and surveillance. AI will likely give us something equally contradictory. Miraculous breakthroughs alongside catastrophic manipulation. The question isn't whether the technology itself is good or bad. It's whether we'll finally break the cycle of deploying powerful tools without consent, without restraint, without thinking through the people we're going to hurt.

The answer probably isn't regulation, though we need that too. It's literacy. It's critical thinking. It's the hard work of understanding how we're being influenced and by whom. It's recognizing that you're not immune to conspiracy thinking just because you're skeptical of conspiracy theories.

Six hundred years of evidence suggests we're not going to learn. But watching Deja Vu suggests we could.
