Flock Safety is making moves to curb police abuse of its automated license plate reader cameras, introducing mandatory oversight features that should have been standard from the start. The company announced several changes this week that address years of criticism from privacy advocates and community members who've watched as officers allegedly used the technology to stalk ex-partners, track abortion seekers, and monitor interstate travel for legal marijuana purchases.

## Overdue Accountability Measures

The updates include making the Audit Assistance feature mandatory for all users. This tool flags "abnormal search behavior" and locks out suspicious users pending administrator review. Police officers will now be required to enter case codes to conduct searches, theoretically limiting personal queries, though administrators can override this requirement in certain scenarios.

CEO Garrett Langley told The Verge he realized the company "got this one wrong" and acknowledged that requiring the audit feature was obvious. It's hard not to point out that this realization comes after at least five years of documented officer-related offenses, including stalking incidents dating back to 2021.

Flock is also reducing data retention periods for its Automated License Plate Reader (ALPR) technology from 30 days to one week for new customers. Existing customers can keep their current retention periods, which the company describes as "democratically approved."

## Why Communities Are Fighting Back

These measures address only one layer of the problem. The ACLU and Electronic Frontier Foundation have consistently accused Flock of misleading the public about what its cameras actually do, claiming they're far more sophisticated than simple license plate readers. Reports have documented Flock data being shared with ICE, despite CEO Langley's defensive responses about state enforcement priorities.

Public backlash has been severe and creative. Communities across the country have responded by vandalizing, destroying, or stealing Flock cameras. One Minnesota town watched its entire stock of cameras disappear overnight, and the police department announced it wouldn't replace them. Los Angeles law enforcement severed ties with the company entirely.

Langley, who was previously combative with critics and even called a camera-tracking website "terroristic," has since apologized for that language and appears to be taking a softer approach. "All I'm asking for is for a fair shot," he told The Verge, acknowledging it took him longer than it should have to understand "the sheer impact of the company."

## A Broader [Tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) Problem

But fair shots require earned trust, and Flock hasn't done much to earn it. The company is 10 years old next year, and these safeguards are coming only after years of documented misuse. The fact that Langley framed mandatory oversight as something he "got wrong" rather than something he should have anticipated from the beginning speaks volumes.

These policy changes are positive steps, but they're insufficient for addressing the fundamental concerns about mass surveillance infrastructure. Even with better oversight, the core issue remains: should these cameras exist in the first place, and what does it mean for privacy when law enforcement has access to tracking technology this powerful?

The vandalism and theft of cameras isn't random destruction. It's communities exercising their agency where institutions have failed to protect their privacy. Whether Flock's new safeguards will be enough to rebuild trust is an open question, but the fact that they're only coming now suggests the company may have already lost the battle in the court of public opinion.
