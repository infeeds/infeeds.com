Chess.com is making a bold move beyond its signature game. The platform that built a community of nearly 300 million members is now launching Classic Games, a new platform featuring 11 additional titles including backgammon, checkers, go, hearts, mahjong, shogi, sudoku, solitaire, xiangqi, and a Scrabble alternative called word. The public beta launches this Saturday on web and mobile apps for iOS and Android.

The expansion addresses a gap in the gaming world. According to Albert Cheng, the company's chief growth officer, Chess.com's cofounders kept hearing from people running mahjong nights or local go clubs who wished for a Chess.com equivalent for their favorite games. "The way that Chess.com has grown this community of almost 300 million members has not really happened for a lot of these other types of classic games," Cheng explained in an interview with The Verge.

## How Chess.com Selected Its New Games

The selection process wasn't random. The team used a combination of community feedback, market research, and internal expertise. Each game on the platform shares a common trait: they offer "some sort of lifelong journey of learning and a community." Strategy games like shogi and xiangqi share chess's appeal, while others like sudoku and solitaire take the platform in new directions.

Each title includes the familiar Chess.com experience: lessons, leaderboards, and rating systems. Multiplayer games feature bot opponents using the same AI characters from the main platform. Yes, Martin the bot remains the lowest-rated across all games.

## AI's Role in Rapid Expansion

Here's the remarkable part: Chess.com built and launched 11 new games using a team representing less than 1 percent of the company. AI played a crucial role in this achievement. The team used AI primarily for coding tasks, employing "almost entirely the same approach" they used when launching Gambit, their gambling-free poker platform earlier this year.

"Because these games are built on top of the Chess.com code base and design system and bot characters and rating systems, it's a huge advantage and allows us to move very quickly alongside AI to bring these types of games to market with a very small team," Cheng said. The existing infrastructure meant each new game could be developed faster than starting from scratch.

But don't mistake this for an AI takeover. The company deliberately kept humans in charge of creative decisions. "The look and feel of it is very carefully crafted by humans, and we're proud of that," Cheng emphasized. Art and design remain firmly in human hands, with AI serving as an accelerant for coding rather than a replacement for creativity.

## Chess and AI: A Long History

Chess.com's comfort with AI might stem from a unique history. [Gaming](https://infeeds.com/tags/gaming) and AI have been intertwined since Garry Kasparov faced IBM's Deep Blue in 1996. "We are fortunate in some ways that AI and chess have been intermixed for 30-ish years," Cheng noted. This decades-long relationship meant the company's founders built AI integration into their DNA from day one, over 20 years ago.

This perspective matters because the broader gaming industry faces tension over AI usage. While Chess.com uses AI to accelerate development, the company is actually hiring more artists and designers. "On the art and the design side, the ratios are shifting a decent amount," Cheng said. The company has shipped more major features in Chess.com itself recently, learning from Classic Games' faster development cycle.

## What's Next

Meanwhile, Chess.com insists chess remains its primary focus. Daily active users hit all-time highs, though Cheng declined to share specific numbers. "Chess is more and more so in the public zeitgeist than I've ever seen it," he said.

The company isn't finished expanding. Cheng hinted that "a lot more" games could arrive in the future. Whether Chess.com becomes a destination for all classic games or remains chess-first with complementary titles remains to be seen, but one thing's clear: the platform is betting that community and quality matter more than quantity when building digital game spaces.
