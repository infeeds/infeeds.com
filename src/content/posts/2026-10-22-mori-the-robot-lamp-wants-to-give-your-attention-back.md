You come home exhausted. The door closes behind you, and in the corner of your eye, a small mushroom-shaped lamp perks up. Its head rises to reveal animated eyes and a diamond-shaped nose that's actually a camera. It jiggles because it sees you. This is Mori, the latest experiment from OKA, a Cambridge and Shenzhen-based startup betting that what your home needs isn't another smart assistant, but a quiet, observant companion.

Mori stands nearly 12 inches tall and costs $649 for the Founder's Edition, which includes two years of subscription service. If you miss that window, the standard edition runs $799 without any subscription included. That's a steep price for a robot that deliberately refuses to do most of what modern smart home devices do. It won't control your lights, play your music on command, or tell you the weather.

## A Creature of Attention, Not Action

Instead, Mori watches. It listens. Using local AI algorithms, it understands context through its camera and microphone, deciding when to interact and when to fade into the background. CEO Hua Wang calls this "Attentive Intelligence." The robot glows in colors inspired by rain, forests, fire, and wind. It dances to your music. It guides breathing exercises. It plays nature soundscapes, but only if you subscribe.

Wang, who previously led operations at Chinese video-sharing app Meipai and cofounded the now-defunct Bondee social network, describes Mori's purpose simply: reconnecting humans with nature and with each other. "All gadgets try to capture more of your attention," he says. "Mori gives your attention back to you."

The robot has memory. It remembers you over time, acting shy around strangers and forgetting people it hasn't seen in a month. It notices when you've been doomscrolling for two hours and might nudge you toward some calming soundscapes. During a phone call or argument, it goes silent. It's less a tool and more a presence, though privacy concerns naturally emerge from a [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) device with a built-in camera and microphone in your living room.

Wang addresses this directly: raw images and audio are processed locally and discarded. Facial data is encrypted on-device and never uploaded. The camera shuts off when Mori retracts into lamp mode, and the microphone can be disabled with a double-tap.

## The Pet Comparison

Chris Harrison, director of Carnegie Mellon's Future Interfaces Group, draws a comparison to Sony's Aibo robot dog from 1999. Early digital companions lacked the crucial ingredient: modern AI capable of understanding and reacting to human emotions. Harrison notes that Mori's limited feature set doesn't diminish its value. "People extract a lot of pleasure out of taking care of pets and interacting with them that I wouldn't say are necessarily more sophisticated," he says. "Are we holding [Mori] to an unrealistic bar?"

It's a fair point. We don't expect much from a goldfish beyond existing in our space, yet the companionship registers. Whether Mori achieves that emotional resonance for $649 remains unclear. The startup plans to release five experimental "Artifacts" over its lifetime. Mori is the first, and future versions could connect to Oura Ring data or Spotify, eventually supporting voice commands. For now, though, it's deliberately limited.

## The Real Question

Wang frames Mori's appeal to younger generations feeling isolated in their homes, people who leave their television on just to fill the emptiness. That's a real problem in [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech)-saturated modern life, where connection paradoxically increases while loneliness persists. Whether a robotic mushroom that dances and plays wind sounds addresses this meaningfully depends entirely on the user.

Stock is limited to a few hundred units for the Founder's Edition. That scarcity might be smart marketing, or it might reflect genuine supply constraints. Either way, it signals that OKA isn't betting on mass adoption. They're targeting people willing to pay premium prices for an intentionally constrained experience, people seeking presence over productivity, observation over operation.

The real test isn't whether Mori can fetch or tell time. It's whether, in an age of relentless digital demands and algorithmic optimization, a robot that simply watches and occasionally offers calm sounds can feel like enough.
