Dressed sharply in a black three-piece suit and yellow bow tie, 11-year-old Lubelo Mthenjana took the stage at Cape Town's College of Magic with practiced confidence. As he made paper clips vanish and reappear, conjured sweets from thin air, and left the audience breathless, something remarkable was happening beyond the illusions: a young person from a township marked by violence was discovering that he could transform his community through art.

The College of Magic, nestled in a peaceful suburb of Cape Town, stands as Africa's only dedicated school for young stage magicians and illusionists. Every Saturday morning, about 190 students ages 10 to 18 stream through the Victorian mansion to learn card tricks, juggling, escapology, mentalism, and theatrical production. But this isn't just about sleight of hand. It's about sleight of circumstance.

## Breaking Apartheid Barriers Through Magic

When David Gore founded the school in 1980, South Africa was gripped by apartheid's brutal racial segregation. State schools were divided by race. Public spaces enforced separation. Yet from day one, the College of Magic was multiracial. "Our organization was illegal," Gore recalls matter-of-factly. "This was an opportunity for young people from all backgrounds to come together around a common interest in magic, to learn about each other and understand each other."

Nearly 50 years later, in a city still deeply divided along racial and economic lines, the college remains a beacon of integration. Some students arrive in fancy cars. Others spend an hour in collective taxis. But once they're inside, they share the same equipment, classes, stages, and food. About 80% of students cannot afford the six-year course and are sponsored through private donations.

## More Than Just Tricks

Multiple studies confirm what Gore has long understood: magic develops self-esteem, curiosity, lateral thinking, and problem-solving abilities in young people. A 2021 study found that children who learned magic tricks showed greater improvements in creative fluency than those completing similar art activities. Even watching magic stimulates cognitive flexibility and unconventional thinking.

For Lubelo, who lives in Makhaza township about 40 minutes away, magic has "opened up a new world." He practices constantly at home, juggling potatoes while his mother cooks, and has chosen his stage name: Spade Boy. More importantly, he's become an ambassador in his community, persuading friends to join despite local fears that magic is associated with witchcraft.

"We don't do dark magic," he explains with the wisdom of an 11-year-old. "We just do the kind of magic that makes somebody smile."

## A Refuge From Violence

Lubelo's mother, Yandiswa, credits the college with boosting her son's confidence while keeping him occupied "away from this environment and everything that's happening outside." South Africa experiences nearly 60 murders per day, with Cape Town's townships among the hardest hit. Gunshots are a regular soundtrack to Lubelo's childhood. Boys his age are drawn into drug use and dealing. The magic community itself wasn't spared: just weeks before the article was written, the school held a memorial service for Duma Mgqoki, a 22-year-old former student and volunteer teacher killed in a shooting.

Gore acknowledges the weight of this reality. "On a weekly basis, some of our young people come to us saying they've experienced crime on a firsthand basis," he says. Yet his vision remains undaunted. He sees magic as portable hope, a pocket-sized art form that requires no wealth or expensive equipment. Most critically, he sees today's young magicians as tomorrow's agents of change.

After Lubelo's winning performance at the annual junior magic contest, his friends surrounded him with fist bumps and backslaps. Within minutes, they were outside launching colored leather balls skyward in a juggling competition, rain beginning to fall. A 14-year-old unicyclist pedaled through the drizzle with absolute certainty: "Even when the storm comes, even when the world ends, I'll never stop unicycling."

That same determination, forged through magic lessons and community, might be exactly what South Africa needs.
