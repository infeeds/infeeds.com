## Political Theater Ends as Cases Collapse

Outgoing Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton has quietly dropped high-profile lawsuits against two doctors who were among the first in the nation to face criminal accusations for providing gender-affirming care to transgender youth. The dismissals came Friday, just as Paxton prepares to leave office following his failed Senate campaign.

The cases against Dr. May Lau and Dr. M. Brett Cooper represented some of Paxton's most visible conservative victories during his tenure. Both doctors were labeled "scofflaws" and "radical gender activists" in press releases that dominated news cycles. Now, those same cases have evaporated without explanation.

## The Convenient Enemy

Dr. Lau's attorney Nicole LeBoeuf didn't mince words about what happened here. "Dr. Lau did nothing wrong, but she was a convenient scapegoat for the Attorney General's Office, which issued press release after press release about its case, a case that has now collapsed," she said.

The timing tells the story. Paxton's loss in his Senate run coincided almost immediately with the dismissals. These weren't cases he believed in deeply enough to defend under a new administration. They were political weapons, deployed during an election cycle when messaging about [transgender issues](https://infeeds.com/tags/health) could move needles at the ballot box.

Consider the numbers. Republicans spent an estimated $183 million on television ads focused on transgender-related attacks during the midterm elections, with Texas's Senate contest alone consuming $58 million of that spending. Paxton's cases against these doctors provided perfect ammunition for that messaging machine. Actual legal merit seemed almost irrelevant.

## A Pattern of Political Prosecutions

This isn't an isolated incident. Paxton's office has quietly shelved numerous lawsuits since his Senate loss, including cases against doctors prescribing abortion medication across state lines, the Council on American-Islamic Relations, and migrant shelter operators. The common thread? Every single one generated headlines and energized Paxton's political base.

Dr. M. Brett Cooper maintained throughout that he "acted lawfully and in the best interests of his patients," and now the evidence suggests he was right. Dr. Lau moved her entire medical practice from Dallas to Oregon because of this lawsuit. Meanwhile, one of the three doctors originally targeted, Dr. Hector Granados, was quietly cleared by Paxton's own office, which conceded he hadn't broken the law.

## What This Means for Medicine

These dismissals reveal something troubling about how legal power operates at the state level. When political energy shifts, so do prosecutions. When an election ends, cases disappear. This creates genuine uncertainty for physicians trying to practice medicine under the law as written.

Texas became the first state to bring lawsuits against doctors over gender-affirming care after its ban took effect in 2023. The cases were supposed to represent genuine legal concerns about statutory violations. Instead, they appear to have been prosecutorial theater designed to energize a political base and generate media coverage.

## Looking Forward

Democrat Nathan Johnson takes over as Texas Attorney General in January, and the political winds have already shifted. What Paxton built up as a signature achievement now crumbles. The doctors he pursued get their professional reputations back and their careers unburdened by state harassment.

But the damage lingers. Major hospital systems across the country have already begun restricting gender-affirming care, even in states where it remains legal. That happened because of messaging like Paxton's, because of lawsuits and press releases and the political ammunition they provided. The real victims here aren't politicians or prosecutors, they're patients caught between medical science and partisan warfare.

When legal cases can be weaponized so easily and then discarded just as quickly, everyone loses faith in the justice system itself.
