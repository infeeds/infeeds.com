Apple is grappling with what CEO John Ternus describes as "unprecedented" memory pricing, a challenge that's reshaping the company's product strategy and profit margins. During the company's Q4 2026 earnings call, Ternus didn't mince words about the severity of the situation, warning investors to expect "even higher memory costs" in the December quarter ahead.

The memory crisis isn't just a backend accounting problem. It's directly impacting customers trying to get their hands on Apple's latest products, particularly the recently launched iPhone Duo, the company's first foldable device. Launched at $1,999 with a 5.4-inch outer display and 7.6-inch inner screen, the iPhone Duo sold out almost immediately during preorders.

## Supply Constraints and Timeline Uncertainty

When asked about when supply and demand might reach equilibrium, Ternus offered little comfort. "We're working hard to get it to as many customers as quickly as possible, but at this point, we're not able to predict when supply and demand will be in balance," he told Reuters. Some customers won't see their iPhone Duo orders fulfilled until 2027, a stark reality for early adopters willing to pay premium prices.

Apple did make a limited number of devices available in physical stores at launch, but inventory remains extremely tight. The combination of memory cost inflation and manufacturing bottlenecks has created a perfect storm for [supply chain](https://infeeds.com/tags/supply-chain) management.

While addressing these supply issues, Ternus also tackled concerns about durability. Reports of cracked screens on the iPhone Duo have circulated, but according to Ternus, "a very small percentage" of units have been returned for this issue. Apple is replacing affected devices, suggesting the company is confident in its ability to stand behind the product despite early quality concerns.

## Pricing Strategy in Response

Apple's response to these pressures has been aggressive price increases across its entire [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) product lineup. The iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max saw particularly notable bumps at launch. Ternus acknowledged that while these increases are "partly offsetting" the memory cost explosion, they're also putting pressure on consumer demand at a time when the company needs volume.

Despite these headwinds, Apple posted record-breaking iPhone revenue of $57.58 billion during its fourth quarter. Notably, this was achieved without even offering a standard iPhone 18 model, suggesting that the company's premium positioning strategy is working, at least for now.

The memory pricing situation raises a larger question about the sustainability of Apple's current trajectory. If memory costs continue climbing while the company attempts to pass those costs to consumers through higher prices, there's a natural ceiling to how much the market will bear. Every dollar increase in iPhone prices risks pushing price-sensitive customers toward competitors.

There's also the matter of how this affects product development cycles. When component costs spike unexpectedly, engineers and product managers face difficult decisions about feature prioritization. Do you invest in the next generation of innovation, or do you focus on cost optimization to maintain margins?

Apple's execution so far suggests the company is walking this tightrope effectively, but the unprecedented nature of current memory pricing means the playbook from previous cycles may no longer apply. The iPhone Duo's delayed availability is a visible reminder that even Apple's massive supply chain infrastructure has limits.
