Nearly 300,000 people die from drowning each year worldwide, yet most of these deaths are entirely preventable. The World Health Organization has just launched a comprehensive technical package called PROTECT to help governments and communities implement seven proven strategies to reduce this staggering toll.

Drowning remains among the ten leading causes of death for children aged 5 to 14 years globally. What makes this crisis particularly acute is geographic inequality: more than 90% of drowning deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, where access to water safety resources and swimming education is severely limited.

## Climate Change is Making Waters More Dangerous

Climate change is adding urgency to drowning prevention efforts. Extreme weather events like floods, storms, and heatwaves are becoming increasingly frequent, creating higher risks of drowning in vulnerable communities. The data is sobering. In the United Kingdom, research found that drowning deaths increase by 7.2% for every 1-degree Celsius rise in temperature. Risk becomes nearly five times higher on days when temperatures exceed 25 degrees Celsius compared with days below 10 degrees.

These statistics underscore how environmental factors amplify existing vulnerabilities. As we see temperature records shattered year after year, drowning prevention must become a central component of climate adaptation strategies.

## The PROTECT Framework: Seven Evidence-Based Strategies

The PROTECT package translates a broader Global Strategy for Drowning Prevention into actionable policy guidance for decision-makers. The framework covers seven interconnected domains aimed at strengthening safety around water. These include making water transport safer, teaching school children to swim, and preparing communities for floods.

Dr Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, WHO Director-General, emphasizes that "every drowning is a tragedy, and most are preventable." He notes that drowning prevention isn't the responsibility of a single ministry but requires everyone's participation. The lives saved become the reward for coordinated action across sectors and communities.

## Ghana Leads the Way

The WHO launched this package in Ghana, recognizing the country's growing political commitment to [health](https://infeeds.com/tags/health) and safety initiatives. Ghana faces its own drowning crisis: approximately 1,100 people die from drowning annually, with children and young adults disproportionately affected. That's roughly three deaths every day.

Ghana's leadership on this issue sends a powerful message to other governments worldwide. By adopting evidence-based policies and frameworks like PROTECT, nations can meaningfully reduce drowning deaths in their communities.

## A Decade of Partnership Bearing Fruit

The launch builds on more than ten years of collaboration between WHO, governments, and global partners. Bloomberg Philanthropies has been particularly instrumental, partnering with WHO since 2014 to support drowning prevention development. Dr Kelly Henning, who leads Bloomberg Philanthropies' public health program, states that "this framework will save lives while building on the progress governments around the world are making."

Bloomberg's support has helped governments worldwide implement proven, effective strategies that demonstrably reduce drowning deaths. The organization's commitment reflects a broader recognition that [prevention](https://infeeds.com/tags/prevention) investments in health infrastructure and education yield substantial returns in lives saved.

## Unite to Turn the Tide

This year's World Drowning Prevention Day, falling on July 25, carries the theme "Unite to turn the tide." The slogan captures an essential truth: preventing drowning requires coordinated action across governmental boundaries, economic sectors, and community lines.

Drowning deaths occur in rivers, lakes, wells, domestic water storage containers, and swimming pools. Each setting requires tailored approaches, yet common principles unite effective prevention work. Universal swimming education, accessible rescue equipment, flood preparedness, and safer water infrastructure form the foundation.

As climate change intensifies extreme weather events and temperatures climb, the urgency of drowning prevention becomes impossible to ignore. The question isn't whether we can prevent these deaths but whether we have the collective will to implement known solutions.
