Geothermal energy has always seemed like the perfect grid solution: abundant, reliable, and zero-carbon. But getting to it has been the hard part. The industry knows what it needs: drill fast and drill deep. It's just a matter of actually doing it without destroying your equipment in the process.

Enter Mazama Energy, a Khosla-backed startup that's tackling one of geothermal's thorniest problems with an elegant solution called Carbon Chill. Instead of using traditional mud-based drilling fluids to cool the bit and clear cuttings, Mazama injects liquid carbon dioxide down the drill string. As it travels deeper and heats up, the CO2 becomes supercritical, then expands through a rocket nozzle-like valve near the drill head, cooling the electronics and bit in the process.

"Our rate of penetration increased 20% over the already very high rates we were achieving," Mazama's co-founder and CEO Sriram Vasantharajan told TechCrunch in an exclusive. That might not sound dramatic until you realize drilling accounts for 25% to 30% of total power plant costs. Faster drilling means dramatically cheaper wells.

## Why This Matters for the Grid

The stakes here are enormous. A National Laboratory of the Rockies report suggests geothermal could generate between 26.5 and 57 terawatts of electricity in the U.S. alone. If just 10% of the conservative estimate got developed, it would more than double American peak generating capacity. That's not incremental; that's transformational.

Big [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) companies have already noticed. Google and Meta are actively exploring geothermal deployments, and hyperscalers are planning facilities set to come online in the 2030s. They need reliable, always-on power sources, and geothermal fits the bill perfectly.

The key unlock is accessing super-hot rock, where temperatures exceed 752 degrees Fahrenheit. At those temperatures, water becomes "supercritical," a strange state that carries five to ten times more energy than water from traditional geothermal wells. Tap into that, and developers can generate more electricity from fewer wells, bringing costs down further.

## The Drilling Challenge Nobody's Solved Yet

Here's where traditional methods hit a wall. Most geothermal drillers use mud, a mix of water and other compounds, to keep drill bits cool. But mud has limits. As you go deeper into super-hot rock, it simply can't dissipate enough heat. The drill bit's onboard electronics, which keep the tool on course, start to fail.

You could use high-temperature-rated electronics, but Vasantharajan said they'd be cost-prohibitive, essentially erasing any savings from faster drilling. That's a showstopper for the economics.

Mazama's carbon dioxide approach sidesteps this entirely. The physics are clever: CO2 absorbs enormous amounts of heat as it transitions to supercritical state, then releases it explosively as it expands through the nozzle. The result? A drill bit and electronics that stay cool enough to function at depths and temperatures where mud simply fails.

## What's Coming Next

The startup has already proved the concept works. Last October, Mazama drilled a well in Oregon reaching 629 degrees Fahrenheit. A more recent well surpassed 700 degrees Celsius, putting them firmly in super-hot territory. In September, the company raised $135 million in funding led by Centaurus Capital and Doerr Capital, giving it serious resources to scale.

Vasantharajan says that by next year, Mazama hopes to complete a single well capable of generating up to 15 megawatts of electricity. For context, that's substantial power output from one hole in the ground.

"What was just a dream or a quest, I can tell you with confidence is now a promise," Vasantharajan said. "We see a path towards this commercial super-hot rock geothermal."

If Mazama and competitors like it can crack faster, cheaper, deeper drilling, they could fundamentally reshape how America thinks about baseload power. The 2030s could look very different from today's grid.
