When Apple first stuffed an iPhone processor into a laptop, skeptics had every right to be concerned. A smartphone chip handling desktop-level computing seemed like a recipe for frustration. Yet the MacBook Neo proves that sometimes unconventional decisions pay off.

The Neo launched at $700, which looks brilliant now that the MacBook Air jumped $200 to $1,300 just three months later. Those tariffs and memory price spikes everyone saw coming are finally here, and Apple's bet on repurposing mobile silicon suddenly makes financial sense. This laptop won't win any performance awards, but for the money, it's surprisingly competent.

## The A18 Pro Does More Than Expected

Under the hood sits an A18 Pro, the same processor found in the iPhone 16 Pro but scaled down slightly. The Neo gets five GPU cores instead of six, a concession to the $700 price point. That's inferior graphics performance right out of the box, especially considering you're potentially pushing pixels across a 13-inch display with more demanding workloads than a phone typically handles.

Here's what's wild: it mostly doesn't matter. During testing, the Neo juggled multiple browser tabs, a streaming show, photo editing software, and games without breaking a sweat. This actually reveals something important about modern iPhone chips - they've been overpowered for smartphones for years. Mobile processors have had more computational muscle than we ever really needed just for scrolling social media.

But there are real walls you'll hit. The 8GB of RAM becomes a serious bottleneck with advanced tasks. Try editing 4K video with special effects and the laptop slows to a crawl. Several demanding applications outright refuse to run without an M1 processor, including games like Assassin's Creed Shadows. The Neo can technically handle vanilla 4K editing, but don't expect a smooth experience.

## Connectivity Takes a Step Backward

The port situation is genuinely disappointing. You're getting a 10Gbps USB 3 port, a 480Mbps USB 2 port, and a headphone jack. That's it. While you can connect a 4K monitor at 60Hz through the USB 3 connection, there's no Thunderbolt support and no daisy-chaining capabilities. Compare that to the MacBook Air's 40Gbps USB 4 ports with Thunderbolt 4, plus dedicated MagSafe charging, and the Neo feels antiquated in the connectivity department.

This matters more than it might seem on the surface. Your external device options shrivel significantly. Photographers and videographers accustomed to high-speed data transfer will feel the loss immediately.

## Should You Wait for the Next Generation?

The real decision point arrives when you consider longevity. According to Bloomberg's Mark Gurman, Apple plans a 2027 refresh with an A19 Pro chip and 12GB of RAM. That extra RAM could smooth out several of the current model's pain points, though 12GB still trails the 16GB baseline found on today's best laptops.

Here's the thing: you can't upgrade RAM in modern MacBooks after purchase. This makes planning ahead crucial. Apple's already pushing AI features in macOS 27 that require M3 processors minimum, and the second-gen Neo will only get a partial slice of what's coming. Heavy creative work like AutoCAD will remain out of reach. Still, waiting for the A19 puts you in a meaningfully better position for the next five years.

If you need a [Mac](https://infeeds.com/tags/mac) right now, the current Neo won't disappoint for everyday tasks. The [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) is new enough that app support won't dry up anytime soon, given the March 2026 launch date. Hunt down a refurbished model to squeeze out extra savings.

The real question hanging over the Neo isn't about today's performance - it's whether $700 now looks foolish compared to $1,000 later for a machine that'll actually keep pace with where computing is heading.
