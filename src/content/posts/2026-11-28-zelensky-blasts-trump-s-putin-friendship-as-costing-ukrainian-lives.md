Volodymyr Zelensky has launched a sharp critique of Donald Trump's approach to Russia, calling it "strange" that the US president describes Vladimir Putin as a friend while Ukrainian soldiers die on the battlefield.

In a candid BBC interview from his central Kyiv office, Ukraine's leader expressed deep frustration with American policy, arguing that delays in delivering vital anti-ballistic missiles are directly costing lives. He also questioned why Trump posted a photograph of himself embracing Putin, a move that sent troubling signals about US commitment to Ukraine's victory.

"Of course it's up to the president of the United States, but... it's strange," Zelensky said in English. "He's an aggressor, and a killer... He can't be a friend. Just cannot be."

The comparison was stark: Zelensky drew a parallel to calling Adolf Hitler a friend during World War Two, underscoring the gravity of treating an aggressor with such warmth.

## The Gap Between Words and Action

Trump's two envoys, Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, have been attempting to broker peace talks between Russia and Ukraine. However, Zelensky suggests they're more sympathetic to Moscow's position than Kyiv's. Kushner recently described Putin as a "grand master of politics" and praised his "restraint" for not deploying nuclear weapons, language that struck many observers as oddly deferential to an invading power.

The [news](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) of peace talks in Abu Dhabi earlier this month offered little hope. Zelensky's assessment was blunt: "The more we know, the more problems."

Meanwhile, Russia has hardened its negotiating stance rather than softening it. The latest round of talks produced no concrete progress, and uncertainty remains about whether European negotiators will even participate in future discussions.

## Missing Weapons, Missing Support

The most urgent concern for Ukraine isn't diplomatic rhetoric, it's military capacity. Russian ballistic missiles are striking with increasing frequency and lethality, targeting power stations and infrastructure. While Ukrainian forces have improved their ability to destroy about 70% of Russia's fastest drones, the real crisis involves interceptor missiles.

Zelensky voiced exasperation over delayed US deliveries of Patriot systems. "The deficit of these missiles - that is people's lives," he said. He suggested American focus on the Middle East has meant less support for Ukraine when it's needed most.

There's also ongoing frustration over Starlink restrictions in occupied Ukrainian territory. Zelensky expressed uncertainty about whether SpaceX or the US government bears responsibility for these limitations, describing the situation as a [news](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) of "ping pong" between agencies.

## A City Under Siege

Kyiv itself is bracing for a brutal winter. Russian strikes on bridges over the Dnipro River appear designed to split the capital in two. Power cuts have forced businesses to close, and residents have begun evacuating. Yet Zelensky remains defiant.

"People can go, but we will try to defend our capital in any way," he said. "We need our capital. Without a capital, you don't have a country."

When pressed on Ukraine's recent long-range strikes against Russian targets, including warships and oil refineries, Zelensky defended the operations as militarily justified. He acknowledged the danger but insisted Ukraine coordinates such moves with its partners.

## The Neutrality Problem

Zelensky's core argument cuts to the heart of his frustration with American policy: neutrality toward an aggressor is itself a form of support for that aggressor. When you refuse to pressure the invader to halt their invasion, you're essentially enabling the violence.

Trump has responded by framing his friendly approach to Putin as pragmatic diplomacy. He suggested that hostility would only entrench positions and prevent deals. Yet five years into Russia's full-scale invasion, with civilian casualties at all-time highs, the strategy of befriending the aggressor looks less like pragmatism and more like abandonment.

Ukraine urgently needs allies who will push Russia hard, not coax it gently. The question remains whether America will become that ally or continue down the path of equivocal engagement.
