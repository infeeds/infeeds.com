## A Rare Voice Silenced

Macao has sentenced pro-democracy former lawmaker Au Kam San to 12 and a half years in prison, marking the casino hub's first major prosecution under its national security law since 2009. The 69-year-old was convicted of subverting state power, breaching confidentiality, and establishing contact with overseas organizations to commit acts endangering national security.

The case is significant not just for what it says about Macao, but for what it signals about Beijing's broader approach to [news](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) dissent across its territories. Au spent 20 years as a lawmaker before stepping down in 2021, making him one of the few consistent critics of government policy in a legislature dominated by Beijing loyalists.

## The Trial Nobody Saw

What makes this conviction particularly troubling is how it happened. The trial was held entirely behind closed doors in September, with the court citing national security interests as justification for the secrecy. No public record of specific allegations, evidence, or testimonies exists. No observers could verify the fairness of proceedings.

Jorge Menezes, a Portuguese lawyer practicing in Macao, pointed out that the court statement claimed Au "acknowledged some facts," which could mislead people into believing he confessed. "From what I see, he did not plead guilty. Otherwise, they would have said," Menezes explained. "This is misleading and unfortunate. It violates his dignity."

Au's international counsel, Michael Polak, was more direct: "A sentence like that for someone of that age is a sentence for life." At 69, Au could spend his remaining years imprisoned in Macao.

## What He Actually Did

The prosecution alleged that Au helped overseas organizations spread what they call false information between 2023 and 2024, attempting to induce foreign countries to take hostile actions against China and Macao. He's also accused of leaking confidential information obtained during closed-door committee meetings about gaming license renewals to overseas groups.

But here's the rub: before his arrest, Au's actual public activities consisted mainly of Facebook posts and interviews criticizing government measures. Many observers considered his critiques mild. He wasn't calling for violent revolution or organizing street protests. He was doing what most would consider basic political discourse.

Au became a lawmaker because he saw social problems requiring people to speak out. He was a teacher first, an organizer of Macao's Tiananmen Square commemorations, and someone who believed his city needed more open and democratic society.

## A Message Broader Than One Man

Critics argue this case reflects Beijing's emphasis on security and intolerance of dissent, even though Macao's pro-democracy movement has never approached the scale or visibility of Hong Kong's. The timing and nature of the prosecution sends a clear message: challenging authorities carries severe consequences.

Macao lawyer Menezes suggested this case was "used to send a message to Macao residents that it was about anyone who challenges the authorities." He also noted that Macao functions as "a sort of laboratory" for Hong Kong, testing how far Beijing will go in suppressing political opposition.

Au's family remains devastated. His wife, Virginia Cheang, questioned the entire process in a public letter: "From a closed-door trial to a public verdict, what kind of operation is this? It's a political trial and it's a groundless persecution." Au's daughter in London told the Associated Press that her father is "a peaceful family man" and that "it just doesn't feel like a safe space anymore being in Macao."

Portugal's government and the European Union have been urged to demand that Au, a Portuguese citizen, be allowed to leave Macao. His case raises uncomfortable questions about what autonomy these special administrative regions truly retain, and whether [news](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) speaking your mind in China remains possible even in places supposedly governed differently.
