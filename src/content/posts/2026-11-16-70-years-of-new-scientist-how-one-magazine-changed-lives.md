When New Scientist first hit newsstands on November 22, 1956, nobody could have predicted it would become a cultural phenomenon that shaped entire careers and sparked scientific passions across generations. Yet here we are, seven decades later, and the stories keep coming. Readers young and old have opened up about how this publication changed the trajectory of their lives in ways both profound and wonderfully unexpected.

Peter Hall was just 13 when he scraped together a shilling from his Saturday morning jobs to buy that first edition. Seven decades later, he's still a subscriber. His journey took him around the globe in scientific discovery, partly fueled by New Scientist's coverage of the Jodrell Bank telescope opening in 1957. That's not just longevity; that's a lifetime of curiosity sustained by one magazine.

## From Classroom Discovery to Global Impact

Many readers encountered New Scientist in school libraries, where it became a gateway drug to [science](https://infeeds.com/tags/science) careers. Frank Evans discovered it at Fettes College in Edinburgh and credits the magazine with helping him think laterally throughout his entire life. Anne Rodgers found an article about Einstein's relativity in the 1960s that blew her young mind, leading her to study physical sciences at university. The ripple effects are staggering: one teacher at Malvern College was so convinced of New Scientist's value that he bought the first issue with his own money and handed it to a promising student, Peter Inman, who went on to spend 60 years in the UK Institution of Civil Engineers.

But perhaps the most delightful story belongs to Mel, who read about Sputnik and Laika the space dog, then got so inspired he built a module to send a maggot into the sky aboard a 3-pence firework. The maggot returned unharmed and was repurposed as fishing bait. That's the kind of hands-on enthusiasm that separates genuine scientific curiosity from passive consumption.

## When Magazine Meets Life Purpose

The influence extends far beyond career choices. A group of 12 older women in Marlow meet monthly to discuss challenging articles on quantum computing, AI, and cosmology. They could have given up on understanding complex topics, but instead they teach each other, look up unfamiliar concepts, and refuse to let age diminish their intellectual engagement. That's radical.

Terence Watts, a psychotherapist, read about Benjamin Libet's experiments on consciousness and the gap between brain decisions and awareness. He spent two years exploring how to apply this insight to therapy, and now psychologists, counsellors, and psychiatrists worldwide use his methods. One article. Two years of work. Decades of global impact.

## The Unexpected Connections

New Scientist even facilitated human connection. Andrea Cohen met her husband Simon on the magazine's dating website in 2012 after 50 years of searching. They've been happily married for 11 years and bond over the magazine's cartoons. Meanwhile, Stephen Wang discovered biology through a single Tesco magazine cover in 2019, which set him on a path that led to a science degree and now a postgraduate degree pursuing research that might help fight human illness.

## A Magazine That Listens

The contributors to The Last Word column represent something special. Mike Follows has been answering questions since 1997 and describes the experience as one of the few ways he achieves mindfulness. One question about whether pigeons sweat led to a BBC Breakfast appearance while he was scuba-diving in Thailand. Tom Eastwood had his wasp question featured in a bestselling book.

[Trustworthy science journalism](https://infeeds.com/tags/science) isn't just nice to have; it's essential infrastructure for a functioning society. New Scientist proves that when you respect your readers' intelligence, invest in clarity, and genuinely engage with curiosity, you build something that lasts seven decades and counting. The question isn't whether this magazine will survive another 70 years, but how many more lives it will transform in ways we can't yet imagine.
