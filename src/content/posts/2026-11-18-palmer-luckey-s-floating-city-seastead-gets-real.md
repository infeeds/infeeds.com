Palmer Luckey, the Oculus founder who sold his VR company to Facebook for billions, has a new passion project that sounds like it came straight out of a sci-fi novel. It's called SLAB, and it's a floating "seastead" designed to last a thousand years while operating as a sovereign city-state free from pesky government regulations.

The concept isn't entirely new in Silicon Valley circles. Tech billionaires like Peter Thiel and Joe Lonsdale have long championed seasteading as the ultimate libertarian fantasy: build a floating structure far enough out in the ocean, and you can essentially create your own country with your own rules. But Luckey's version comes with a specific twist that raises more than a few eyebrows.

## What Exactly Is SLAB?

According to Luckey, SLAB is designed to be cheaper than building on land while avoiding "state/local laws that cripple critical American industries." The industries he lists include metal processing, medical research and treatment, desalination, oil refining, and chemical engineering. An AI render shows a massive artificial island with office buildings, apartments, refineries, and what appears to be a data center.

The timeline matters here. Luckey claims this project predates the recent AI boom, and he's annoyed that journalists plan to call it a "floating AI datacenter." In a tweet that might be the cringiest thing he's ever posted, he wrote: "It is way cooler than that, way older than that, and I hate looking like a hype-chasing consensus loser, so now you have to read this tweet."

Fair point, maybe. But the optics are still complicated. A sovereign floating structure designed to dodge regulations around medical research and chemical engineering naturally invites scrutiny about what kind of work actually needs that much freedom from oversight.

## The Manifest Destiny Problem

Perhaps most revealing is Luckey's stated goal of "reviving manifest destiny and westward expansion." That phrase carries a lot of historical baggage, and it's hard not to notice the irony. We're talking about escaping American law to make "America stronger," as Luckey framed it in his original post.

The [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) billionaire frames SLAB as a "relief valve" for outdated regulations. But there's a fundamental question lurking here: if regulations exist to protect public health, environmental safety, or worker welfare, what exactly changes when you float away from them? The answer, it seems, is everything for certain industries that find compliance costs prohibitive.

Luckey isn't shy about his ambitions either. He runs Anduril, a defense contracting firm, and has mentioned plans for breeding military dogs. SLAB fits neatly into his broader vision of libertarian innovation without institutional constraints.

## Why This Matters Now

Luckey only went public with SLAB because journalists from Semafor (Liz Hoffman and Reed Albergotti) were about to write about it anyway. His preemptive disclosure feels like damage control, though the underlying project remains remarkably audacious.

The only other real-world seastead that gained notoriety was Sea Island, which the piracy site Pirate Bay once considered as a potential base of operations. That should tell you something about the libertarian seastead ecosystem.

Whether SLAB actually gets built is almost beside the point. The project reveals how tech's wealthiest figures think about sovereignty, regulation, and their place in society. They don't see themselves as citizens bound by the same rules as everyone else. Instead, they see regulations as obstacles to be circumvented, not frameworks to be engaged with democratically.

For now, SLAB remains a render and a provocative idea. But if Luckey's track record with [startups](https://infeeds.com/tags/startups) tells us anything, he's probably serious about trying. The real question isn't whether he can build it, but whether international maritime law would ever recognize his floating island as a sovereign state in the first place.
