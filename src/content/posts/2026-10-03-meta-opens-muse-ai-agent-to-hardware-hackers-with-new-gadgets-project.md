Meta is taking its Muse personal AI agent beyond software and into the physical world. The company just launched Muse Gadgets, an open-source project that invites developers and tinkerers to build custom hardware that connects to Muse, the AI assistant that already books travel, fills out forms, and shops on behalf of users.

The initiative arrives with open-source firmware and a Linux software development kit (SDK), giving creators the tools to experiment. Meta even threw in some project ideas to spark imagination: a color e-ink display for Muse or loading it onto a stick that plugs into a TV's HDMI port. The possibilities extend far beyond these suggestions, though.

Developers can grab a low-cost Raspberry Pi or an off-the-shelf ESP32 board, then wire up Muse to "your displays, buttons, sensors, actuators, and whatever else you've got lying on your workbench," according to Meta's documentation. There's also a Discord channel set up for community support, because no hackathon is complete without a vibrant support network.

## Meta is already eating its own cooking

Nat Friedman, head of product at Meta's Superintelligence Labs, posted about a device the company built itself: Muse Home Link. This USB-C-powered gadget lets Muse connect to a home network and communicate with smart devices like speakers and smart TVs. It's a compelling proof of concept that shows what's possible when you combine an AI agent with physical hardware.

Meta manufactured 5,000 of these Home Links and is giving them away free to Muse subscribers while supplies last. Given that Friedman's announcement post garnered nearly 30,000 views in just hours, those freebies likely vanished fast. Shipments are expected in a few weeks.

## A broader play for AI dominance

Muse Gadgets might seem niche, but it fits squarely into Meta's ambitious strategy to make Muse indispensable across multiple user types. The company isn't satisfied with positioning Muse as just another AI chatbot. It wants Muse embedded in how people work, shop, and manage their lives.

Earlier this week, Meta rolled out Muse for Small Business, offering free access with usage limits and integrations with tools like Shopify, Dropbox, and Slack. The company also created a new business unit called Meta Enterprise Platform to accelerate AI penetration into corporate environments. This is a company playing the long game, building [Muse](https://infeeds.com/tags/meta) into something far larger than a consumer novelty.

The Gadgets project targets a specific but growing audience: developers who want to customize how they interact with AI. It's a clever way to extend Muse's reach into the maker community while generating organic innovation. When hackers and tinkerers build useful implementations, Meta benefits from the attention and the free development.

There's also a symbolic message here. By open-sourcing the firmware and SDK, Meta signals that it's serious about building an ecosystem rather than a walled garden. Compare this to some competitors in the [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) space that jealously guard their infrastructure. Whether this openness reflects genuine philosophical alignment or smart competitive positioning remains an open question.

The real test comes next: will developers actually build interesting things with Muse Gadgets? Will we see creative applications emerge that Meta didn't anticipate? Or will this become another well-intentioned but underutilized open-source project gathering dust on GitHub?

What's clear is that Meta is betting hard on making AI agents less abstract and more tangible, more embedded in the objects and systems we already use. The question isn't whether Muse will matter, but rather how many different hardware endpoints it will eventually reach.
