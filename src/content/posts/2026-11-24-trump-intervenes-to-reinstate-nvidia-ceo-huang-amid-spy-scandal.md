## The Unlikely Comeback

In a stunning reversal that has left Washington reeling, Nvidia's board of directors reinstated CEO Jensen Huang just days after explicitly stating they had no intention of doing so. The turnaround came after direct intervention from President Donald Trump, who claimed on Monday that he personally "convinced" the tech giant's leadership to bring Huang back.

Huang had resigned on November 12 following reports of extramarital affairs, including one allegedly involving a woman with ties to Chinese intelligence. The initial board decision to reject his reinstatement lasted barely a week. By Monday, everything had changed.

## Money Talks

The financial stakes explain much of the urgency. Nvidia's stock price collapsed following Huang's resignation, contributing to a broader market correction. When the board announced his reinstatement, the company's shares jumped 31% in a single day, adding nearly $1 trillion to Nvidia's market value. The stock market, it seems, cares more about leadership continuity than scandal.

Trump didn't shy away from admitting his role. "People say, is it a threat to have him? No, it's a threat to lose him," the president told CNBC. He framed the decision as essential to American economic growth, noting that Nvidia's performance has been crucial to US market performance for years.

But here's where things get uncomfortable. Recent financial disclosures show Trump has purchased Nvidia stock worth between $23 million and $86 million through multiple investment accounts since returning to the White House. That's 85 separate purchases. He's also sold shares, netting between $3.5 million and $10.2 million in gains. The timing of his intervention to save a company in which he holds substantial financial interests has not gone unnoticed.

## Congressional Fury

Lawmakers from both parties are furious. This isn't partisan posturing. Republican Representative Don Bacon, who chairs a House subcommittee on cyber security, minced no words: "We cannot say we want to win the AI race and then give China our best technology."

Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries called the decision "indefensible, irresponsible and incomprehensible." Senator Elizabeth Warren, set to chair the Senate Banking Committee, denounced the reinstatement as "reckless" and demanded answers about which government officials pressured the board.

Even GOP Senator Tom Cotton sounded the alarm, warning that the Nvidia-Megaspeed controversy "is just the tip of the iceberg." (Notably, the Commerce Department announced it would blacklist Megaspeed, the Singapore-based firm where the woman in question served as CEO, though this came only after Huang's reinstatement was announced.)

## The National Security Question

Board Chair Harvey C Jones claimed the reinstatement posed no national security risk and that the board received "input from senior levels of the US government" supporting the decision. But what exactly was that input, and from whom? Congress is demanding transparency, and frankly, the lack of clarity is damning.

Prof Stuart Russell from UC Berkeley acknowledged that the Nvidia board must have faced "enormous pressure" from the White House. The professor noted that Nvidia's stock performance has been critical to US economic growth, suggesting the administration worried markets would crater further without Huang's return.

Huang is scheduled to testify before Congress in a closed-door interview on December 1 and before the Senate Banking Committee two days later. These hearings will determine whether this comeback stands or becomes another chapter in a scandal that reveals troubling priorities in how America's leaders weigh security concerns against market stability and personal financial interests.

The question isn't whether Huang is innocent or guilty. It's whether the most powerful person in the world should be using that power to influence corporate decisions in companies where he holds millions of dollars in stock.
