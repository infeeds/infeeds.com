Mark your calendars for August 12, 2026, because astronomy enthusiasts are in for a treat. Not only will the world witness the first total solar eclipse since 2024, but this cosmic event also aligns with the annual Perseid meteor shower. If that wasn't enough to get you excited, the eclipse will actually last longer than usual thanks to lunar perigee, when the moon sits at its closest point to Earth and appears larger in our sky.

However, not all viewing locations are created equal. If you want the absolute best seat in the house, you'll need to travel to either Spain or Iceland. While North America had the spotlight in 2024, this eclipse is all about Europe.

## The Prime Viewing Locations

Western Iceland and northern Spain will offer the most dramatic experiences. In Iceland, Látrabjargi will host the longest duration of totality, and the full eclipse is also visible from Reykjavík, Ísafjörður, and other western coastal cities. But here's the thing: Spain genuinely outshines Iceland for eclipse viewing.

Spain's advantages are twofold. First, the eclipse's path crosses significantly more land there than in Iceland, meaning you'll have more spots to choose from for your perfect vantage point. Second, August weather in Spain runs 20 to 30 degrees warmer than Iceland, which matters when you're standing outside staring at the sky for hours.

In Spain, major cities like Bilbao, Burgos, Palma, Santander, Valencia, Valladolid, and Zaragoza will all experience totality. A small sliver of Portugal near the northeast border, specifically Montesinho Natural Park in Bragança, also falls in the path. Russia and Greenland will see totality too, but those locations require specialized expeditions and serious travel commitment.

## What About North America?

Don't despair if transatlantic travel isn't in your budget. Northern parts of the United States and eastern Canada will see a partial eclipse, though coverage varies dramatically. New York City will only catch 9 percent coverage, while St. John's in Newfoundland will experience a much more impressive 53 percent. The partial view is still worth witnessing, especially if you're interested in [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) solutions for observing celestial events.

## Protecting Your Eyes: No Exceptions

Here's where things get serious. You can only safely look directly at a solar eclipse during the brief period of totality, and even then, only if you're in the path. Looking at any other time causes severe, irreversible eye damage. Since the United States won't experience totality anywhere in the country, every American viewer needs eye protection the entire time.

Forgot about those fancy sunglasses or binoculars. They won't cut it. Your only options for direct viewing are specialized eclipse glasses or solar viewers approved by the American Astronomical Society. If you're planning to photograph the eclipse, you'll also need special lens covers.

## DIY Viewing Options

The good news? Creating an indirect viewer costs almost nothing and requires minimal effort. A pinhole projector is the classic choice, and NASA provides crafting directions right on their official website. You can also raid your kitchen for household items with small holes, like a colander, or simply use your own hands in a criss-cross pattern.

The technique is simple: position yourself with your back to the sun, let light pass through the holes, and watch the eclipse project onto a surface like a wall or the ground. It's low-tech, effective, and safe.

Whether you're booking flights to Spain, exploring partial eclipse options in Canada, or fashioning a pinhole projector from household items, the 2026 solar eclipse offers something for everyone interested in [astronomy](https://infeeds.com/tags/astronomy). The question isn't whether you'll experience this eclipse, but rather how you'll choose to witness it.
