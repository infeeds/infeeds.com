Picture a typical day at your office. People typing at desks. Sitting in meetings. Sitting in the kitchen grabbing lunch. You can go through an entire workday without standing up once. That should worry any [business](https://infeeds.com/tags/business) leader trying to extract real output from a team.

After nine years building and scaling BetterMe, I've learned one lesson I keep returning to: movement is one of the most underrated drivers of productivity in business. Yet most companies treat it as a wellness perk instead of infrastructure. A meditation stipend or gym reimbursement doesn't fix a workday architected around sitting. Rethinking the workday itself does.

## The Productivity Science Is Real

Think about a lazy, couch-bound Sunday. After a day like that, do you feel energized? I don't. I feel sluggish, slower, and even simple decisions get harder. That's the exact state employees end up in after spending eight hours moving from one chair to another.

The qualities we value most at work, creativity, mental clarity, problem-solving, all rely on good blood flow and oxygen reaching the brain. Movement supports both. When you move, circulation improves, more oxygen reaches the brain, and cognitive function sharpens. The McKinsey Health Institute estimates that improving employee health and well-being globally could create up to $11.7 trillion in value each year. Much of that comes from higher productivity and less presenteeism, people who are technically at work but nowhere near their full capacity.

A study from Oxford University's Saïd Business School found workers are 13% more productive when they feel better at work. Movement is one of the most direct, cheapest levers a leader has to move that number.

## How I Structure My Day Around Movement

Staying physically active isn't a wellness habit for me. It's one of the tools that keeps my energy and focus where they need to be. Instead of squeezing in a workout when I can find time, I've built movement into the structure of my day.

In the morning, I start with at least an hour of movement. Most days it's Pilates or yoga. A few times a week I get out for tennis or golf. By the time I sit down to work, I already feel switched on. During the day, I schedule short breaks for stretching, walking or a quick exercise. Even ten minutes can reset your energy. In the evening, I slow things down with quieter mindfulness practices: meditation, chanting mantras and Pranayama breathing. It reduces stress and clears mental noise.

None of this is unusual. What's unusual is treating it as non-negotiable and letting it shape how the workday gets structured.

## Making Movement the Default

Culture isn't purely top-down, but it helps when leadership sends the first signal. When a team sees a founder step away for a walk between meetings, others feel like they can do the same without overthinking it.

From there, specific changes make it easier for everyone. Make your environment movement-friendly with adjustable desks, whiteboards and supplies placed farther from workstations, and open space to stretch or walk between calls. Rethink meeting culture. Not every meeting needs to happen sitting down. Turn 1:1s into walks. Make stand-up meetings actually stand at a standing table. Make cameras optional on calls so people feel free to move while listening.

Create shared rituals. A quick stretch before any meeting running more than an hour. A short exercise after 30 minutes of sitting. A Friday step-count check-in with a weekly leaderboard. Small habits, shared by the team, give the day structure.

Make it social. It's easier to stay consistent when you're not doing it alone. Sign up for a step challenge as a team. Turn [team building events](https://infeeds.com/tags/business) into something active, badminton instead of drinks. Set up a Slack channel to share progress and small wins. Block movement on the calendar. If it's not scheduled, it's easy to skip. When people see those blocks on each other's calendars, stepping away stops feeling like something only they do.

The point isn't to turn the office into a gym. It's to stop pretending that eight hours of sitting produces the best version of the people you hired. Strong teams don't just brainstorm together. They move together.
