Six months have passed since the U.S. and Israel launched their campaign against Iran, and the outcome defies initial expectations. The regime that was supposed to crumble has instead dug in deeper, tightening its grip on power even as the country's economy spirals into its worst crisis since the 1980s.

The opening assault was devastating. Between February 28 and April 8, intensive bombing wiped out Iran's air force, destroyed its air defenses, and crippled its navy. Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, who had ruled for over three decades, was killed on the first day along with dozens of other top leaders. Yet somehow, the regime survived.

## Military Capability vs. Strategic Leverage

Iran may have lost much of its traditional military power, but it discovered something more valuable: leverage. By controlling the Strait of Hormuz, a waterway critical to global oil and gas supplies, Tehran has weaponized geography itself.

The shipping disruption has sent shockwaves through the global economy, driving up fuel and fertilizer costs worldwide. Despite this, the Trump administration rejected an Iran-Oman deal that would have temporarily opened the strait, threatening to even bomb Oman, a U.S. ally. The standoff continues, with nearby Gulf nations desperately seeking alternate routes that remain inadequate.

The human toll has been severe. The [World Health Organization](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) reports 3,375 Iranians killed and over 33,000 injured. U.S. military deaths stand at just 18, though analysts question how long the U.S. can sustain such operations given depleted munitions stockpiles.

## The People Pay the Price

While regime officials contemplate negotiations behind closed doors, ordinary Iranians face a grinding humanitarian crisis. The economy is expected to shrink by 5.4% this year. Inflation has soared above 80%, and the rial has collapsed in value.

A 30-year-old blogger in Tehran describes buying groceries on credit, her conversations with friends reduced to hunting for the cheapest food. "Getting sick is no longer an option," she says, noting that a doctor's visit could mean financial ruin.

Medicine shortages compound the misery. Sara, a 50-year-old asthmatic, watches her generic inhaler price jump from $5 to $15, and it's increasingly unavailable despite being domestically produced. These aren't abstract statistics; they're the daily calculus of survival for millions.

The regime responds to desperation not with relief but with repression. The United Nations reports a surge in executions, with at least 56 people executed on national security charges since mid-March, including 27 linked to recent protests. Human rights groups estimate more than 7,000 were killed and tens of thousands arrested during crackdowns earlier this year.

## Can Change Come From Within?

Yet cracks may be forming in the regime's foundation. According to Alex Vatanka, a senior fellow at the Middle East Institute, there's growing consensus among Iran's ruling class that endless conflict threatens long-term survival. Senior figures including Supreme National Security Council head Mohsen Rezaei and Revolutionary Guard chief Ahmad Vahidi are reportedly backing a return to talks, though quietly.

The Trump administration's new Operation Economic Outcast aims to further isolate Iran through ramped-up sanctions, potentially pushing the regime toward even harder lines rather than compromise. Negotiations initiated after the April ceasefire have already stalled.

"At the end of the day, it's unrealistic to expect that you can undo 47 years of bad blood overnight," Vatanka observes. "The best-case scenario is there won't be a clear-cut winner. There'll be a compromise, and that's the best thing we can hope for."

But street protests alone won't force that compromise, he warns. Change requires peeling off regime members, engineering defections, and building political alternatives. Simply hoping that hunger and suffering will topple a government willing to execute its own citizens is fantasy.

Six months in, Iran's regime survives not because it won a war, but because it controls the instruments of repression and enough strategic leverage to make withdrawal costly. The question isn't whether the regime will fall, but whether the Iranian people can endure the cost of its survival.
