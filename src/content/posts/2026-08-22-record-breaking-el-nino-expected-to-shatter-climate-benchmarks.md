The tropical Pacific is experiencing an El Nino so intense that climate scientists are struggling to find adequate comparisons. James Hansen, the renowned climate scientist at Columbia University, calls it simply "off the charts," noting that conventional measures have already surpassed all previous records in the instrumental data era, despite the phenomenon still having months before reaching peak intensity.

## Measuring the Unmeasurable

El Ninos occur when warm surface waters spread eastward along the equator, disrupting global weather patterns. But quantifying their strength isn't straightforward. Hansen argues the best metric is the heat anomaly in the upper 300 meters of the equatorial Pacific Ocean, which already exceeds the legendary 1997-98 super El Nino that dominated headlines and disrupted weather worldwide.

The more commonly cited Niño3.4 index, which measures sea surface temperature anomalies in a specific Pacific rectangle, tells a slightly different story. Adam Scaife at the UK Met Office suggests this index hasn't quite matched the 1997-98 peak, sitting at about 2 degrees above normal rather than the 2.5 degrees recorded during that event. Yet the daily Niño3.4 value hit 2.75°C earlier this month, creating genuine uncertainty about whether we're already in record territory.

Daniel Swain at the California Institute for Water Resources splits the difference, arguing that Hansen has the stronger case. He notes that using running averages with long lags masks the rapid evolution of this event. On the measure that matters most for near-term impacts, Niño3.4 values are clearly the highest recorded for this time of year and expected to remain so through the end of 2024.

## A Historical Reckoning

What emerges from these competing perspectives isn't confusion so much as agreement on the fundamental point: this is genuinely exceptional. Zeke Hausfather at Berkeley Earth describes it as the third strongest event on record under standard metrics, though he notes that upper ocean heat typically precedes surface temperature extremes, suggesting worse is coming.

Kevin Trenberth at the National Center for Atmospheric Research is more unequivocal: "I agree that this El Nino is the biggest on record." Adam Scaife, despite earlier caveats, declared in a statement that he has "never seen an El Nino signal this intense in our forecasts," predicting 2026 will far exceed recent experience of such events.

But the truly sobering perspective comes from Hayley Fowler at Newcastle University, who suggests this developing super El Nino could exceed not just recent records but historical ones as well. She draws a chilling parallel to the 1877-1878 El Nino, which triggered widespread famines and caused approximately 50 million deaths, representing 3-4 percent of the global population at that time, concentrated across Asia and South America.

## What Comes Next

The difference between then and now matters enormously. We have far better predictive capacity, early warning systems, and food storage capabilities. Yet we also have a much larger global population and greater climate instability layered on top of El Nino oscillations. The intersection of these forces remains genuinely uncertain.

What's unambiguous is that this event will serve as a natural experiment in how modern climate systems respond to extreme Pacific warming. Scientists worldwide are watching intently, knowing that whatever unfolds over the next 12 months will reshape our understanding of El Nino intensity and global climate sensitivity.
