## A Rooster Row in Maine

What started as a heartfelt gesture for an autistic daughter has escalated into a federal court showdown over free speech and governmental overreach. David and Sara LeBlanc installed giant metal roosters outside their restaurant in Ogunquit, Maine, not to drum up business, but as a deeply personal symbol of their love for their daughter Pyper. Yet the town saw something entirely different: advertising.

The roughly 6-foot-tall tin statues became such a comfort to Pyper that her face lights up every time she sees one, according to her parents. But Ogunquit's town government disagreed with the family's interpretation. Rather than celebrate a parent's creative attempt to support their child's [health](https://infeeds.com/tags/health) and wellbeing, officials treated the roosters as a regulatory violation.

## The Battle Begins

The town issued multiple fines and eventually granted the couple a disability variance. Sounds reasonable? Not quite. The variance came with a catch: the roosters had to be attached to a railing. When the LeBlancs opted to chain them to posts instead, more fines followed.

This isn't just about stubbornness or technical violations. The lawsuit filed this week by the LeBlancs, represented by the Institute for Justice, highlights something much more troubling: selective enforcement. Other local businesses display oversized lobster buoys, antique farm equipment, and large tiki statues without facing similar penalties. The roosters, the family argues, are being singled out because of what the town believes they represent, not what they actually are.

"This is governmental discrimination based on the content of speech and the identity of the speaker," the lawsuit states, and it's hard to argue with that logic.

## A Precedent for Protection

The LeBlancs' legal team has momentum on their side. In 2025, the Institute for Justice won a similar case in New Hampshire involving a bakery owner and a large mural. A judge sided with the bakery owner when the town of Conway tried to regulate a doughnuts-and-treats mural as a sign. The reasoning? The town was infringing on free speech rights by dictating what counted as protected expression.

If the same principle applies in Maine, Ogunquit has weak footing. The town can't have it both ways: allowing one business to display large decorative objects while banning another's based purely on content interpretation.

## The Broader Story

What makes this story even more compelling is what happened after the family removed the roosters. Their original statues were adopted by local residents and businesses. Supporters worldwide began buying their own roosters to display in solidarity. The rooster became a symbol not just of one family's love, but of resistance to bureaucratic overreach.

Sara LeBlanc's statement captures the heartbreak: "Every time Pyper spots another rooster in someone's yard, her face lights up. This town has embraced her. We wish the town government would, too."

The town clerk didn't respond to requests for comment, which is its prerogative but hardly inspires confidence in the town's position. When you can't articulate why your enforcement is fair and reasonable, silence often speaks volumes.

## What's Really at Stake

Beyond the roosters, this case raises fundamental questions about government authority and free expression. Can towns arbitrarily decide what counts as "advertising" versus "decoration"? Can officials enforce rules selectively based on subjective interpretations of content? And most importantly, should a parent's effort to support their child's emotional wellbeing be subject to municipal bureaucratic approval?

The answers matter for more than just Maine. [Free speech](https://infeeds.com/tags/free-speech) protections depend on consistent, viewpoint-neutral application of law. When governments pick and choose which expressive acts to regulate, they've already crossed a line.

Ogunquit may have thought it was managing signs and advertising. Instead, it became the unlikely antagonist in a story about what happens when rigid rule-following collides with human compassion.
