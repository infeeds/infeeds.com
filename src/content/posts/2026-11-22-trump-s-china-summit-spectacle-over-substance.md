President Trump's high-profile trip to China this week delivered compelling theater but left deeper questions about what was actually accomplished. The imagery was carefully curated: children chanting warm welcomes, Xi Jinping greeting Trump on the tarmac, even a surprise appearance by Vladimir Putin. Yet beneath the pageantry, experts wonder whether the summit addressed any of the real tensions between the world's two largest superpowers.

Robert Daly, director of the US-China relations program at University of Maryland's School of Public Policy, offers a sobering assessment. Xi Jinping's primary goal was performative, Daly explains. The Chinese leader wanted his people to see him as a confident world leader hosting other nations in a position of respect. It echoes an ancient Chinese saying about the 10,000 kingdoms bowing to the center.

## The Hug Heard Round the World

The physical embrace between Trump and Xi became the summit's most memorable moment. Americans might interpret such warmth as genuine friendship, but Daly cautions against reading too much into it. For the Chinese public, it humanizes a leader often criticized for being too rigid and controlled. Xi rarely expresses emotion or personal feelings, even about fellow Chinese citizens. As a leader of 1.4 billion people, he maintains an image of being above personality politics.

What's particularly telling is what didn't happen. Despite Trump's repeated public compliments toward Xi, calling him a great and wise leader, Xi offered no reciprocal language in return. He simply pocketed the praise with an enigmatic smile. The Chinese government readout of the three-way meeting contained only bland platitudes about peace and friendship. In Chinese political culture, that restraint carries meaning: if you refuse to return a compliment, you win.

## Putin's Awkward Third Wheel Status

Vladgebeurtenissen Putin's presence added complexity to an already delicate dance. The Russian president apparently orchestrated his own invitation, seeking to maintain his status as a world leader despite leading a nation increasingly isolated for its invasion of Ukraine. Yet Putin didn't receive the same ceremonial treatment as Trump. No airport greeting, no public bear hug, though he's met with Xi roughly 50 times before.

This distinction matters in diplomatic circles where symbolic gestures carry enormous weight. Some Russians expressed online that Putin was dissed, though others defended the arrangement. Meanwhile, [China continues enabling Russia's war machine](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) through oil purchases and dual-use technology exports that keep Moscow's economy and military functioning.

## The Nuclear Carrot and the Stick

There was one tangible outcome: Trump secured public agreement from Xi to discuss nuclear weapons. It's a thin accomplishment, but potentially significant. China isn't ready to negotiate actual weapons reduction. The country plans to build its arsenal until achieving parity with the US in roughly a decade. What Beijing did agree to is discussing "reducing nuclear risks" and nonproliferation measures. That vague language cracks a door open.

Daly sees this as worthwhile but insufficient. The larger worry is precedent. If Trump sets an expectation that American presidents must shower Xi with compliments and avoid discussing American values or concerns about Chinese conduct, what happens when his successor doesn't play by those rules? Previous presidents managed to be pleasant and respectful while still advocating for American interests.

## Performance Over Problem-Solving

All American presidents who've visited China maintained diplomatic courtesy. Trump didn't invent pleasantries in foreign relations. But this summit seemed to prioritize performative friendship over addressing substantive disagreements. The US and China face genuine tensions over artificial intelligence competition, trade disputes, and strategic rivalry. These issues demand more than warm rhetoric.

Daly's concern cuts to the heart of modern diplomacy: we're increasingly focused on how things appear rather than what gets solved. Cameras capture the hug, social media spreads the image, and domestic audiences in both nations see their leader treated with respect. Meanwhile, the real problems remain unexamined and unresolved.

The question now is whether this summit established a new template for US-China relations, one where style consistently trumps substance. And whether that approach actually serves American interests long-term.
