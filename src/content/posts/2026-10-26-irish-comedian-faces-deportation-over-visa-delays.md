Jaishree Malik walks on stage at the Cheeky Monkey comedy club in Dublin and pulls out an envelope. It's a deportation letter from the Irish government. The audience laughs nervously at first, then realizes she's serious. "Don't worry, it's not for you," she quips, before reading aloud the minister's proposal to deport her back to India.

At 25, Malik has become perhaps the only person to transform a deportation order into material for open mic nights. But her situation reflects a far larger problem: Ireland's immigration system has become increasingly harsh and unpredictable, catching skilled workers, students, and asylum seekers in bureaucratic limbo.

Malik moved to Ireland in 2022 from Gurgaon to study for an M.Phil in Literature at Trinity College Dublin. She worked on campus during her studies and after graduation, securing a two-year visa through the Third Level Graduate Scheme. Everything seemed secure until she applied to extend her visa while pending confirmation of a new job offer.

## When Bureaucracy Becomes Absurd

The delay in processing her application cost her the job in February. The department of justice ruled she had no right to work while they considered her case. She submitted additional information to strengthen her application and waited. And waited.

Meanwhile, the government's department of public works offered her a position. She couldn't accept it. The reason? Government bureaucracy wouldn't clear her to work while the government processed her visa application. "It's so absurd," Malik told audiences. "I'm being offered a job by the government that I can't take because of the government."

She began performing at open mic nights to channel her anxiety. The comedy became sharper, more pointed, more true. Some audience members thought she was playing a character until she explained her situation was real. "Your best material comes when your life is falling apart," she reflected.

On 5 October, the deportation letter arrived. Malik will leave Ireland in November, but she's committed to performing until the end. She's shredding her possessions, closing her Irish bank account, and heading to Dublin airport in the early hours of 14 November. Her flatmate, she notes with gratitude, has offered to drive her despite the inconvenient time.

## A Broader Pattern of Hardship

Malik's case is not unique in [news](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) coverage about Ireland's changing approach to immigration. The government has toughened border enforcement and rules on migration, asylum, and naturalisation, citing a housing shortage and strain on public services attributed to immigration. Justice Minister Jim O'Callaghan has explicitly stated authorities wish to curb population growth that is seven times the EU average.

Other victims include Gaza students who were denied visas despite receiving scholarship offers from Irish universities. "We were refused for documents we were never asked to provide, such as a bank statement and proof of intention to return," said a group representing the students. "We were then told to appeal with original documents by post, and as you know, Gaza is destroyed and there is no postal service." These students have escalated their cases to Ireland's high court.

Labour Party legislator Ivana Bacik says the hardening reflects a departure from Irish values. "I believe there's much more compassion and warmth towards inward migration among Irish communities and it's not reflected in the ministry's approach," she stated. The system is losing employers good talent and turning away deserving candidates.

Malik describes her experience as Kafkaesque, referencing the novel about a protagonist trapped by unreasoning bureaucracy. "Why would I read The Trial? I'm living it," she said. Yet she's found power in the absurdity. "When I get up on stage and I make people laugh, it sort of disarms the anger and the political stuff. That is the jester's privilege."

She hasn't fully processed that she must leave. "I don't think it'll hit me until I'm on the plane." She's bracing for reverse culture shock and the peculiar pain of leaving behind a life she'd begun building, friendships that felt like family, and a country that seemed to want her until suddenly it didn't.

Ireland's audiences have been supportive despite her critiques of the system. The real question isn't whether Malik is funny enough to survive in comedy. It's whether a country that claims to welcome talent and diversity actually means it.
