For decades, World War II cinema has offered comfortable moral certainty: resistance heroes fighting against evil, collaborators getting what they deserved. But a new generation of European filmmakers is upending that narrative, presenting the murky reality of occupied Europe through the eyes of those who sided with the occupiers.

This year's Venice Golden Lion winner, 'Woman Unknown', tells the story of Marie, a maid who had a sexual relationship with a German officer during Denmark's occupation. After liberation, she faces violent retribution from resistance fighters, while her wealthy employer who profited from Nazi business dealings escapes unscathed. The film refuses easy answers about who deserves blame.

It's not alone. Emmanuel Marre's 'A Man of His Time' follows a Vichy bureaucrat climbing administrative ranks, while Xavier Giannoli's 'Rays and Shadows' sympathetically portrays Jean Luchaire, a pacifist who became a pro-collaboration propagandist. These aren't fringe projects; they're major productions challenging how we remember the war.

## The Myth We Told Ourselves

Historian Halik Kochanski offers a sobering correction to postwar mythology: "One of the biggest myths people told themselves after 1945 was that Europe liberated itself." Armed resistance? It involved no more than 5% of the population in most countries. The reality was far messier than cinema suggested.

In Poland, Nazi genocide policies and plans to erase Polish nationhood drove resistance as survival. But Western Europe presented different dynamics. Denmark, technically neutral until 1943, faced no martial law until late in occupation. The Netherlands had flat terrain unsuitable for armed resistance. France didn't see widespread dissent until forced labour was instituted in 1942. Most people weren't heroes or villains; they were muddling through.

Many administrators who collaborated genuinely believed they were protecting their populations from worse German brutality. "We are the shield," they told themselves, only discovering too late that this shield would force their own citizens into camps and labour squads.

## The Moral Cost of Resistance

The new films grapple with an uncomfortable truth: active resistance meant risking not just your own death, but everyone around you. When a German Waffen-SS unit murdered 642 civilians in Oradour-sur-Glane in 1944, they were retaliating for one resistance killing. How many people have the right to endanger their entire community?

Cambridge historian Bernhard Fulda is sceptical of modern moral certainty. "If you haven't lived under a dictatorship, how do you know what you would have done?" he asks. This isn't moral relativism; it's intellectual humility. We're shaped by our comfortable distance from totalitarianism, our ability to judge from safety.

Yet resistance took countless subtle forms barely visible in [news](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) headlines. In Norway, mass resignations, strikes, and citizens spamming the education ministry with protest letters sabotaged Quisling's Nazification. Dutch forgers created 80,000 false identity documents, many of their members later executed. Farmhands recorded milk deliveries to track troop movements. These acts weren't glamorous but they mattered.

Photographer Violette Cornelius documented occupation's final year with hidden cameras. Yet even here, ambiguity lurked: some photographers used darkroom skills to forge documents while others had earlier taken portraits for legitimate Nazi identification cards. The line between complicity and resistance blurred constantly.

## Reckoning in an Age of Hypocrisy

Fulda suggests something profound about contemporary audiences: "In the 1980s, the moral righteousness required to say 'you would have supported fascists and I wouldn't' came much more easily. But shaped by the climate crisis, finger-pointing is harder because we know our own lifestyle choices contribute to the problem."

We're all collaborators now in small ways, complicit in systems we oppose. Flying easyJet while claiming environmentalism. Buying from exploitative corporations while lecturing others about ethics. Perhaps the concept of "collaboration" itself has become [news](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) obsolete when everyone is complicit.

These films aren't excusing Nazism or collaboration. They're asking us to inhabit uncomfortable territory where good people made bad compromises, where survival required impossible choices, where moral clarity was a luxury most couldn't afford.

As far-right movements strengthen across Europe, the timing feels deliberately provocative. Yet maybe the real provocation isn't sympathising with collaborators, but recognising how easily we might become them.
