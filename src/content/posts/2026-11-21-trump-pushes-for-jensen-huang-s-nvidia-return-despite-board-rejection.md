President Donald Trump has thrown his weight behind Jensen Huang's potential return to Nvidia, calling for the ousted CEO to be given "a second chance" despite the company's board unanimously rejecting any such possibility.

Huang resigned on November 12 following media reports of an extramarital affair with a woman suspected of being a Chinese spy who may have facilitated the transfer of restricted AI chips to China. The situation has since spiraled into a larger scandal involving multiple affairs and questions about national security.

"You have one of the truly most brilliant minds in the world," Trump told reporters on Friday. "I think that I would love to see Jensen be given a second chance." Trump characterized Huang as "a good man" who simply "made some bad decisions," while urging him to issue "a big apology" and return to lead the company.

## The Board Stands Firm

Unfortunately for Trump, Nvidia's board has made its position crystal clear. Board chairperson Harvey Jones stated during an earnings call that directors unanimously accepted Huang's resignation and have no plans to reinstate him. This creates an awkward dynamic where the President's personal preference directly contradicts the formal decision of the company's leadership.

Interestingly, Huang himself appeared open to returning, posting on X on Tuesday that he offered to come back. But that olive branch was immediately rejected by the board, suggesting deeper concerns beyond the personal scandals at play.

## Xi Weighs In

Trump's comments also contradicted his own Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who had stated earlier on Friday that Jensen Huang didn't come up during Trump's recent talks with Chinese President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen.

Yet Trump claimed otherwise. "I did discuss that with President Xi, and he said, 'Oh, brilliant, brilliant! He's a brilliant man'," Trump recounted. This conversation raises eyebrows about why the President would bring up a disgraced executive with Beijing during diplomatic talks, and it underscores how the Huang situation has become entangled in geopolitical considerations.

The New York Times reported that Alice Huang, the suspected spy involved in the scandal, maintains extensive ties to China's defence industry through family connections and associates. US intelligence officials reportedly knew about these potential espionage links beforehand, making the entire situation far more complex than a simple corporate scandal.

## Broader Fallout

The Huang debacle has also ensnared Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. Republican Senator Tom Cotton of Arkansas told The Wall Street Journal that Lutnick repeatedly ignored warnings about AI chips being smuggled to China before the scandal broke. Trump has publicly backed Lutnick, calling him "a different kind of a guy, very good businessman" and saying he hasn't "lost confidence" in him.

Democrats, who will control Congress starting in January, are now investigating whether Lutnick used his office to benefit Cantor Fitzgerald, the firm run by his sons, by helping them avoid losses related to Nvidia's scrutiny and Chinese company connections. This layer of investigation adds governmental oversight to what might otherwise seem like a private [news](https://infeeds.com/tags/news) matter.

## What It Means

The clash between Trump's preference for Huang's return and Nvidia's board decision reveals tensions between political leadership and corporate governance. While Trump may see Huang's technical brilliance as outweighing personal failings, the board appears concerned about reputational damage and the serious national security implications surrounding the espionage allegations.

Nvidia built Blackwell, its most advanced chip technology, under Huang's leadership, and the company remains critical to America's AI infrastructure. This makes the situation far more than a typical corporate scandal affecting only shareholders and employees. The stakes involve national security, trade policy, and the future direction of crucial technology sectors.

Trump's push to rehabilitate Huang suggests a pragmatic view that talent and innovation matter more than personal conduct. Whether that calculation proves correct may depend on whether the espionage concerns can be adequately addressed and resolved.
