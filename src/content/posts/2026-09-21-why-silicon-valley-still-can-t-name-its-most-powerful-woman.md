At a black-tie gala in San Francisco, Mira Murati took the stage to discuss revolutions. No one listened. When a male Trump adviser followed, the room fell silent.

This moment crystallizes the paradox of power in [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech): women occupy the highest echelons of influence, yet remain fundamentally unseen.

Ask industry insiders who wields the most power in Silicon Valley and you get quick answers. Elon. Jensen. Sam. Peter. Marc. Ask the same question about women and watch the room go quiet. Maybe Gwynne? Lisa? The hesitation is telling.

## The Visibility Problem

Over three months of reporting, a clear pattern emerged. The most powerful women in tech operate across multiple capitals: financial, intellectual, political, and cultural. Yet unlike their male counterparts, they rarely achieve the mononymous celebrity status that commands attention.

Gwynne Shotwell has grown SpaceX into an $800 billion company. Ruth Porat presides over Google's finances as chief investment officer. Dina Powell McCormick shapes Meta's direction from her position as president and vice chair. These are titans by any measure, yet casual industry observers draw blanks when asked their names.

The men, by contrast, have cult of personality. They post a single message on X and sway elections. They deliver manifestos that echo for decades. They command the room without trying.

## What Changed, and What Didn't

Sheryl Sandberg's "Lean In" promised answers. When the book launched in 2013, it articulated workplace realities many women faced: second-class treatment, systemic self-doubt, pay inequality. For nearly a decade, the manifesto felt revolutionary.

But the 2010s revealed the limits of individual hustle. Ellen Pao's discrimination lawsuit against Kleiner Perkins, Susan Fowler's exposé of Uber's culture, and subsequent allegations of sexual harassment forced tech's reckoning. Firms hired investigators and crisis teams. Women suddenly appeared on leadership rosters.

Then came the backlash. "The 2010s was the era of lip service," says Timnit Gebru, who was fired from Google after publishing research on AI bias. "'Look at how many women we have on our team!' And if there was one woman on the team, that woman got advertised to death."

## Power Looks Different for Women

Anne Wojcicki recently bought back her own company after 23andMe went bankrupt. Most investors declined to support her. Yet Adam Neumann can obliterate his company's value and raise hundreds of millions for his next venture.

Wojcicki's mother, Esther, offered a theory about visibility and power. You need cultural capital, she said. You need presence on X. You need noise.

Anne pushed back. She argued that Gwynne Shotwell proves power doesn't require constant platform broadcasting. Shotwell stays heads-down, executing. She jets between Silicon Valley and Washington, regulatory approvals in hand. The general public may not know her name, but every power broker does.

## The Silence on Identity

Here's where it gets complicated. When asked whether the industry has become more welcoming to women, responses vary wildly. Some celebrate incremental progress. Others invoke the backlash to anti-DEI initiatives and note that discussing identity has become radioactive.

"Women don't want to talk about it, either," one founder told me anonymously.

The cultural moment shifted after Trump's 2024 reelection. Elon Musk's Twitter transformed into a platform where [tech](https://infeeds.com/tags/tech) men felt emboldened to share rightward beliefs openly. The industry's brief flirtation with examining its gender problem evaporated.

But underground networks persisted. Ifeoma Ozoma left Pinterest over pay discrimination and helped pass California's Silenced No More Act. Gebru continues investigating AI harms through her independent research collective. Women in tech still organize, still push back, just more quietly.

## The Real Question

The issue isn't whether women belong in power. They're already there. The question is whether the architecture of power itself needs to change.

Male dominance in tech relies partly on a specific formula: visibility plus financial capital plus cultural narrative control. Women leaders often excel at the first two while deliberately avoiding the third, perhaps sensing that the playing field isn't actually level.

Until we stop measuring power exclusively through the male model of celebrity and disruption, we'll keep struggling to name the women who already hold it.
